China to Israel Customs Valuation 2026: Transaction Value, Article 8 Additions & the DDP Declared Value

Duty and VAT into Israel are ad valorem: a percentage of a figure Customs must first fix. That figure, the customs value, is a legal determination, not whatever a seller prints on an invoice. This guide covers how Israel sets it, what must be added, and why DDP shifts the risk onto the seller.

Published 24 November 2026 · Reading time ~7 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Why Declared Value Is a Compliance Question

Exporters sometimes treat declared value as a dial to turn down so a door-to-door quote looks cheaper. Customs does not. Where duty is ad valorem, the customs value is the base the tariff rate applies to, fixed by a statutory sequence of methods. HS classification decides the rate; valuation decides the base. An understated value defers cost rather than removing it: on revaluation the shortfall returns with interest, penalties and storage. On DDP the party quoting the all-in rate is usually importer of record, with no downstream customer to pass it to.

The Legal Base: The WTO Agreement in Israeli Practice

Israel is a WTO member and has implemented the Customs Valuation Agreement, the Agreement on Implementation of Article VII of the GATT 1994. It replaced the older Brussels Definition of Value, based on a notional market price, with a system built on the price actually paid or payable. The aim is a fair, uniform, neutral valuation following commercial reality rather than an official price list. Domestically it sits alongside the Customs and Purchase Tax Tariff under the Customs Ordinance valuation provisions. Declarations are electronic and the commercial invoice is central: buyer and seller details, an HS-consistent description, quantities, prices, Incoterm, origin, currency and signatures.

The Six Methods, Applied in a Fixed Order

The Agreement sets out six methods as a hierarchy, not a menu. Customs works down the list and cannot skip to a convenient number; only when one fails does the next apply. One nuance: methods four and five can be reversed at the importer's request, not the officer's discretion.

Method 1: Transaction Value, Stated Precisely

Transaction value is the price actually paid or payable for goods sold for export to the importing country, plus Article 8 additions. It is the total payment by the buyer to the seller, including third-party payments made as a condition of sale, not just the invoice face. Conditions attach: the buyer must be free to use the goods, the price must not depend on conditions of undeterminable value, no resale proceeds may accrue to the seller without an adjustment, and related parties must not have influenced the price. A genuine discount differs from a retrospective credit.

Article 8: The Closed List of Additions

Article 8 lists what must be added to reach transaction value. The list is exhaustive, no other additions are permitted, and each addition must rest on objective and quantifiable data. If Customs cannot quantify a cost, the adjustment cannot be made.

Certain costs are excluded where distinguishable: post-import inland freight, construction, assembly or technical assistance after importation, and the importing country's own duties and taxes. Buying commissions are not added. On assists, only design work undertaken outside the importing country counts, and general research is not an assist.

CIF, and Why VAT Is Charged on Duty

Israel values on a CIF basis, so customs value includes the goods, insurance and freight to the place of importation. A recurring error is declaring the ex-works or FOB figure and leaving international freight and insurance out on a separate invoice; Customs does not treat them as separate. The tax cascade amplifies it: duty applies to CIF at the HS rate, then import VAT to CIF plus duty and any purchase tax, tax on tax. Israel's standard VAT rose from 17 to 18 per cent effective 1 January 2025. Confirm the current rate before quoting.

Related Parties: When the Relationship Does Not Disqualify

Transaction value assumes two independent businesses at arm's length. Relatedness deserves testing but alone is not grounds for rejecting the declared value. Article 15 defines it narrowly: cross-directorships, partners, employer and employee, control or common control, a holder of five per cent or more of both voting stocks, or family members. The question is whether the relationship influenced the price. Two routes, normally at the importer's option: examine the circumstances of the sale, or compare against test values from identical or similar goods. A Chinese parent selling to its Israeli subsidiary is not an automatic problem, though transfer-pricing documents answer a different test.

Assists, Royalties and Tooling in a China Supply Chain

Two Article 8 items trouble OEM and ODM cargo. An assist is anything the buyer supplies free or at reduced cost for production: tooling, moulds, dies, components, materials, or design work carried out outside the importing country. Its value must be apportioned into the customs value of the resulting imports under a reasonable, accounting-consistent method, even with no money crossing a border. Royalties and licence fees turn on a two-stage test: the payment must relate to the goods and be a condition of sale for export. A trademark fee embodied in the goods is added; an unrelated right is not.

When Customs Doubts the Declared Value

Because transaction value rests largely on importer documents, Article 17 lets Customs satisfy itself as to the truth or accuracy of any statement, document or declaration. A Marrakesh Ministerial Decision on shifting the burden of proof sets the procedure. Customs may ask for further explanation that the declared value is the total actually paid or payable; contracts, remittances and cost breakdowns answer that. If doubt remains, Customs may decide the transaction value method cannot be used. Before a final decision it must give its reasoning and reasonable time to reply, with the final reasoning in writing. Prices visibly below benchmarks tend to trigger the question.

How an Alternative Value Is Set

Once transaction value is set aside, the Customs Ordinance valuation provisions require the statutory hierarchy rather than a figure picked at will. A published district court decision on a personal vehicle import shows the width and the limits of that power: Customs rejected the declared price as unreasonably below the exporting country's market price and set a higher value using a domestic price guide. The court upheld it, noting that at declaration the burden is on the importer to establish accuracy, but once an alternative value is substituted it shifts to Customs, whose authority remains subject to reasonableness, proportionality and the duty to follow the hierarchy and give reasons. A benchmark can replace your invoice if the evidence is thin.

Where DDP Concentrates the Risk

DDP is the default for much China-to-Israel e-commerce because it removes every step from the consignee, but it concentrates classification, valuation and tax responsibility in the seller or its representative. An all-in tax-paid rate far below the statutory burden is rarely efficiency; import VAT at the standard rate is a hard legal cost. Ask how the declared value is built, which Incoterm the invoice uses, and who holds the import file. Release is not the end: Israeli Customs can revisit classification, valuation and origin months or years later on post-clearance audit. The remedy is a complete valuation file retained for the statutory period. Our clearance documents guide covers the declaration pack.

Building a Valuation File Before Sailing

  • Invoice: HS-consistent description, quantities, prices, Incoterm, origin, currency, signatures.
  • Payment evidence: remittances, letters of credit, purchase orders, the sales contract.
  • Freight and insurance: charges to the Israeli port, reconciled to the declared CIF value.
  • Assists and licences: buyer-supplied tooling or free materials with an apportionment method, plus the royalty analysis.
  • Related-party support: transfer-pricing policy and a customs-side comparability analysis.
  • Consistency and retention: invoice, packing list, bill of lading and declaration must agree; keep the whole file for the statutory period.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid in 2026

  • Treating declared value as a discount lever rather than a legal determination.
  • Declaring an ex-works or FOB figure while omitting international freight and insurance on a CIF basis.
  • Assuming free tooling or free design is exempt, when it is an assist to be apportioned.
  • Assuming related-party status is either fatal or irrelevant; the test is price influence.
  • Relying on low-value personal-import thresholds for commercial cargo, or discarding the file after release.
