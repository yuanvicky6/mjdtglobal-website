China to Africa Shipping 2026: Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania & Ghana Lanes Compared

South Africa is where most Chinese sellers open their Africa account, and it is a sensible place to start: one national customs authority, one dominant port, a mature inland hub. But the growth is no longer concentrated in the south. Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania and Ghana now carry a steadily larger share of cross-border demand, and none of them behaves like South Africa. This guide compares the four mainstream non-South-African corridors — which gateway to use, what clearance involves, how long each mode takes, and where the last mile breaks down.

Published 29 September 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Why the South Africa Playbook Does Not Transfer

On a South African lane the customs story is broadly linear: goods enter through Durban, Cape Town or OR Tambo, SARS assesses them, duty and VAT are settled, and cargo moves inland on good roads to established metropolitan markets. Leave that lane and three things change at once.

  • More than one authority. Customs is only the first gate; standards bodies and sector regulators sign off separately, on their own tracks.
  • Pre-shipment conformity. Several of these markets verify product conformity before the cargo ships, so a certificate you planned to arrange on arrival had to exist before loading.
  • Inland reach sets the lead time, not port efficiency. Mombasa and Tema are gateways to landlocked markets reached by road; the corridor behind the port is the variable.

The consequence is simple: a Durban document pack cannot be reused for Lagos or Mombasa.

The East African Corridor: Mombasa and Dar es Salaam

East Africa is the more predictable of the two regions, because its clearance procedures are comparatively well documented and its corridors well trodden.

Mombasa, Kenya is the entry point for the Northern Corridor: cargo clears at the coast, moves inland to Nairobi, then by road to Uganda, Rwanda, South Sudan and the eastern DRC. The Kenya Revenue Authority administers customs, an Import Declaration Form is part of the assessment chain, and the Kenya Bureau of Standards governs conformity, with a pre-export verification programme applying to many manufactured categories. Lining those certificates up in China is usually the difference between a clean release and a fortnight of storage.

Dar es Salaam, Tanzania anchors the Central Corridor, serving Tanzania plus Rwanda, Burundi, Zambia and the DRC. The Tanzania Revenue Authority runs customs and the Tanzania Bureau of Standards covers conformity. The port handles cargo competently, but the inland trucking leg is longer than at Mombasa, so the delivery window widens.

Both ports are served by mainline and feeder vessels from southern China, usually routed through an Asian or Gulf transshipment hub. Transit is measured in weeks, so build slack around regional peaks.

The West African Corridor: Lagos and Tema

West Africa offers the largest consumer demand on the continent and, consistently, the most demanding clearance environment.

Lagos, Nigeria — through the Apapa and Tin Can Island terminals — is the region's biggest single market and its most congested gateway. Terminal dwell and berthing windows are less consistent than anywhere else in this comparison, so the honest transit answer is a range with a generous upper bound. Nigeria also asks more on documentation: the Nigeria Customs Service administers clearance, but the chain involves a Form M registration, a Pre-Arrival Assessment Report obtained before the vessel arrives, and SONCAP conformity assessment for many regulated products. Sector regulators add a further layer for food, cosmetics and pharmaceuticals.

Tema, Ghana is the more orderly West African option, serving Ghana and, by road, Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger. The Ghana Revenue Authority runs customs through a single-window system, the Ghana Standards Authority handles conformity, and health-related categories face their own regulator. It is not frictionless, but it is more predictable than Lagos — a reasonable first West African gateway.

Choosing the Gateway: Five Questions

  1. Where is the end customer, not the port? A slightly less efficient port with a short inland leg often beats a better port with a long corridor behind it.
  2. Which regulator must approve this product? Standards conformity and sector approvals sit outside the customs declaration and cause most avoidable holds.
  3. What dwell buffer can you absorb? On congested West African terminals the buffer is not a margin — it is the plan.
  4. Is this an FCL base load, an LCL top-up or an air shipment? That answer often changes the gateway, not just the rate.
  5. Who clears on the ground, and can they pre-file? A partner who lodges documents before arrival compresses clearance more than any other decision.

Documentation That Actually Gets Cargo Released

Across all four markets, documentation defects — not freight capacity — are the leading cause of delay. Before cargo leaves China, confirm:

  • Commercial invoice and packing list that agree with each other and with the declaration, with clear HS codes and accurate values.
  • Bill of lading or air waybill consigned to whichever entity will clear the goods.
  • Certificate of origin, plus preferential documentation if a trade agreement applies.
  • Conformity certificates where a pre-shipment programme applies, issued before loading rather than after arrival.
  • Importer registration — the local tax ID and importer or customs client number held by the clearing party.
  • Product-specific approvals for regulated categories such as telecom equipment, electrical goods, food and pharmaceuticals.
  • ISPM 15 compliant wood packaging, heat-treated and correctly marked.

One habit is worth stating plainly: never under-declare value. Authorities cross-check declarations against price references, and the penalty is measured in weeks of storage, not just a fine.

Sea, Air or Consolidation?

The mode logic here is the same as anywhere, but getting it wrong costs more because the alternatives are slower.

  • Sea FCL — lowest unit cost for bulk and regular restocking; the default once volumes justify a container.
  • Sea LCL — viable from roughly one cubic metre up. Watch destination charges and free storage days before daily charges begin.
  • Air freight — fast and expensive per kilogram; reserve it for high-value, low-weight goods, launches and genuine stock-outs.
  • Consolidation — a scheduled departure cadence for grouped LCL cargo, giving smaller sellers a predictable rhythm.

A workable structure: sea carries the base load, air fills gaps, and air never quietly becomes the base load.

Last-Mile Reality in Four Cities

  • Nairobi. The most organised of the four, with improving formal addressing and established marketplace pickup points.
  • Lagos. Enormous demand and the hardest delivery environment: dense, congested, reliant on descriptive addresses plus a phone contact. Expect re-attempts as normal.
  • Accra and Tema. Reasonable formal delivery in the main urban corridor; informal addressing takes over outside it.
  • Dar es Salaam. Workable in the city and along main corridors, noticeably slower upcountry.

Two structural realities shape the economics: addressing is often descriptive rather than postal, and cash on delivery still accounts for a meaningful share of orders. Both raise re-attempt rates and stretch the remittance cycle — a working-capital issue as much as a logistics one.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Treating Africa as one market. A Durban quote tells you nothing about reaching Lagos or Kigali.
  • Assuming South African paperwork travels. Each market has its own conformity and registration requirements.
  • Leaving conformity certificates to arrival. Where a pre-shipment programme applies, the certificate must exist before loading.
  • Under-declaring value. Cross-checking is routine; the cost is storage time plus penalties.
  • Booking Lagos without a dwell buffer. Plan against the upper end of the transit range.
  • Making air the default. It flatters your delivery promise and destroys your margin.
  • Skipping the local clearance partner. On congested lanes a competent agent is the difference between days and weeks.
