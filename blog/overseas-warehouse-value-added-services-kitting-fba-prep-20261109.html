Overseas Warehouse Value-Added Services 2026: FBA Prep, Kitting and Relabelling

A warehouse that only stores and ships is a commodity with a rent bill. What separates one 3PL from another is the deliberate work done to a unit before it leaves the rack: the label that lets a retailer scan it, the bag that keeps a claim away, the bundle that turns three slow movers into one sellable SKU. In 2026 that work matters more, because less of it is available at the destination.

Published 9 November 2026 · Reading time ~9 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

What Changed: Prep Moved Upstream

For shipments created on or after 1 January 2026, Amazon no longer prepares or labels units on a seller's behalf. Non-compliant cartons still attract unplanned prep charges, and units that were not properly prepped and labelled are no longer eligible for compensation if damaged or untraceable. Prep is now decided before the carton is closed: at the factory, at a China consolidation hub, or at the overseas warehouse that hands it to the carrier.

The quieter consequence is informational: a warehouse that preps also sees the product, so packaging errors and transit damage are caught before dimensional weight is billed. Value-added work is where a 3PL first learns what you actually sell — which is why the written specification matters more than the rate.

What Counts as Value-Added, and What Is Just Picking

Picking follows an order. Value-added work follows a specification, and usually happens outside the order flow: before inbound, before an FBA shipment, or in a quiet window. The two are priced differently and routinely confused in a rate card.

  • Labelling and relabelling. FNSKU, GS1, retailer-specific or country-of-origin marks, including removal of a wrong code before a correct one goes on.
  • Protective prep. Poly bagging to a thickness and warning standard, bubble wrap, corner protection, desiccant, overboxing.
  • Kitting and bundling. Assembling components into a new sellable unit, sealing it so it cannot be split, and creating the SKU and bill of material behind it.
  • Retail compliance. Carton marking, SSCC-18 pallet labels, pallet build to a routing guide, packing list on the lead carton.
  • Brand and channel layers. Inserts, QR cards, manuals in the right language, price tickets, security tags — plus photographs as evidence.

If a task is not on this list, you are asking a warehouse to invent a process — and invented processes cannot be audited.

Four Places Prep Can Happen

Do the work where labour is cheapest, or where the information is freshest? Cheap labour at origin is wasted if the allocation changes after the container sails; skill at the destination is wasted if the packaging arrives wrong for it.

The usual answer is a split. Apply the barcode that never changes at the factory; keep the label that changes per channel — retailer sticker, price ticket, bundle wrap — for the overseas warehouse. Anything that depends on which promotion is running should not be decided in a Chinese factory in August. Compare options on total landing cost, not the per-label rate: unpacking, repacking, breakage and staging storage sit outside it.

The Rules That Get Units Rejected

Marketplace requirements are easy to read and unforgiving in aggregate: one missing warning line can fail a whole carton. The rules that recur in rejections are consistent.

  • Poly bags. At least 1.5 mil thick, transparent enough for the barcode to scan through, sealed rather than taped, and carrying a suffocation warning where the opening is five inches or more, in readable lettering — conventionally ten-point or larger.
  • Cartons. No more than 50 lb (about 22.7 kg) and no side longer than 25 inches (63.5 cm) unless the carton holds a single oversized item; Team Lift above 50 lb, Mech Lift above 100 lb.
  • Barcodes. The FNSKU must be the outermost scannable code. Two visible codes is a defect in itself, so the FNSKU should cover the original UPC or EAN, or a blank label should.
  • Expiry. Dates on the unit and on the outer carton in the accepted format, with enough shelf life remaining at receipt.
  • Sets. Sealed so components cannot separate, and built so an inner barcode cannot be scanned and stocked as a separate item.

Two failures cost the most, because both look complete on the bench. A relabelling job that leaves the old code half-visible becomes a multiple-barcode defect at the induction line. A bundle sealed with one band of tape separates in a tote, and the components are received as units nobody ordered.

Kitting and Bundling: A New SKU, Not a New Box

A bundle turns three slow movers into one listing with a higher basket value, but it is a new sellable unit with its own identifier, bill of material and inventory consequence. The warehouse must decide which it is recording, and there are two models.

In the first, components are consumed at assembly: stock leaves three component SKUs and arrives as one finished kit. Simple and accurate, but inflexible — a kit built in October for a bundle that changes in November is dead stock that must be unbuilt by hand. In the second, components stay liquid and the kit is assembled to order, which keeps stock flexible but adds an assembly step to every pick.

Most sellers should build the stable bundles and keep promotional ones liquid — postponement applied at the shelf rather than the factory. Write down the bill of material, what happens to a component that fails inspection mid-build, and where a half-finished kit is stored.

Relabelling: The Cheapest Repair in the Warehouse

Relabelling exists because a mistake is cheaper to fix than to ship back. A wrong barcode, a missing country-of-origin mark, an importer address that changed, a price ticket for a channel you no longer supply: each is a unit that cannot be sold as it stands, and each can usually be corrected on a bench in minutes.

The discipline is in the removal, not the application. A label over an old one recreates the multiple-barcode condition marketplaces reject; a label on a curved or dusty surface lifts in transit. Identify the code, remove it completely including residue, scan to confirm nothing readable remains, apply the replacement on a flat clean area away from seams, then scan again while a second person watches. Photograph the first carton of every batch.

Beyond Amazon: Retail Routing Guides

Sellers who add wholesale meet a different rulebook. Retailers publish routing guides that govern carton marking, pallet configuration and height, overhang, which side carries the SSCC-18 pallet label, and how the advance ship notice must be transmitted before the truck arrives. The label is trivial; the data behind it is not, because the SSCC and the ASN have to agree with what is physically on the pallet.

This is genuinely value-added rather than a repack: it needs a warehouse that prints compliant labels, builds to a specified pallet pattern and returns a documented count matching the electronic notice. It also needs inputs you probably have not supplied — the current dated guide, the GS1 prefix and artwork, carton and pallet configuration by SKU including TI/HI, and a named owner for the ASN. Non-compliance is rarely refused at the dock; it is invoiced later as a chargeback.

Inserts, Ticketing and the Brand Layer

Inserts are the cheapest marketing a cross-border seller has and the most common source of quiet errors, because an insert is a pick nobody checks. A batch packed with the wrong card never shows as an inventory variance; it shows as a complaint three weeks later. Give each insert its own code and a line on the pack-out checklist. Price ticketing follows the same logic: a sticker decides which channel the unit belongs to, and cannot be moved without damaging the pack.

A Schedule That Survives an Audit

Disputes are rarely about whether the work happened; they are about what "done" meant. A schedule that answers these questions prevents nearly all of them.

ElementWhat it must state
Scope per operationOne line per operation, with a photograph of a conforming unit as the reference standard
Unit of measurePer unit, per set, per carton or per hour — and which applies when a batch is mixed
MaterialsWho supplies bags, labels, tape and inserts, and who pays for waste and reprints
Yield and accuracyAn expected pass rate per operation, and the rework rule when it is missed
EvidenceWhat is photographed, how long it is kept and who can retrieve it
Change controlHow a specification change mid-batch is priced, and whether work in progress is repriced
Cut-off and capacityThe daily volume committed, and the notice needed for a surge
Scrap and quarantineWhere failed units go, who inspects them and how disposal is authorised

Insist that every operation produces a countable quantity that reconciles with the invoice. Work that cannot be counted cannot be audited, and work that cannot be audited is eventually paid for twice.

Mistakes That Recur

  • Assuming destination prep is still free. Losing claim rights is the expensive part, not the fee.
  • Labelling over a barcode instead of covering it. A half-visible code is a multiple-barcode defect.
  • Building bundles too early. A kit is only an asset if the promotion it was built for still exists.
  • Treating inserts as stationery. An insert is a component and needs a code, a count and a check.
  • Reading the routing guide once. Retailer specifications are revised quietly and enforced by chargeback.
  • No photographs. Without evidence, every dispute becomes a discussion about memory.
