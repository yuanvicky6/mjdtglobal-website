China to Africa & South Africa Shipping 2026: Air vs Sea Freight, Customs & Door-to-Door Delivery

Africa is the last great growth frontier for cross-border e-commerce — a young, urbanising population that is increasingly buying online and by phone. But "shipping to Africa" is really shipping to 54 different markets, each with its own customs, port efficiency and last-mile reality. South Africa is the anchor market for most Chinese sellers. Here's how to move cargo from Shenzhen to Johannesburg, Durban, Cape Town, Lagos, Nairobi or Mombasa without delays, demurrage or a customs hold.

Published 9 September 2026 · Reading time ~9 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Africa Is Not One Market: Read the Lane First

The single most common mistake new sellers make is treating Africa as a single destination. In practice the continent breaks into a handful of very different corridors, and the right freight choice depends entirely on which one you are selling into:

  • Southern Africa — South Africa anchors the region, with Namibia, Botswana, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Mozambique and the DRC largely served through the port of Durban and the Johannesburg inland hub.
  • East Africa — Kenya (Mombasa), Tanzania (Dar es Salaam) and inland Uganda, Rwanda and Ethiopia. Growing fast, with relatively organised clearance.
  • West Africa — Nigeria (Lagos/Apapa), Ghana (Tema), Côte d'Ivoire, Senegal. The highest volume but also the most congested ports and the most paperwork.
  • North Africa — Egypt, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia. Geographically close to Europe and often served on Mediterranean loops rather than "Africa" services.

Because port congestion and import procedures vary so widely, the lane you pick drives everything else — transit time, documentation, cost and delivery reliability. South Africa is almost always the cleanest place to start, which is why it leads this guide.

Air Freight vs Sea Freight: The Core Trade-Off

Most China–Africa cargo goes by sea — ocean freight is overwhelmingly the default because volumes are heavy and margins on consumer goods are thin. Air freight is reserved for high-value, low-weight or time-sensitive shipments. Both are mature out of Shenzhen, Guangzhou and Hong Kong.

For heavier, lower-margin goods, sea freight's lower unit cost dominates. Many sellers run a hybrid: air for fast movers and new product launches, sea for the base stock.

Major Ports and Realistic Door-to-Door Times

Door-to-door adds customs clearance, port handling and last-mile delivery on top of the line-haul. The busiest gateways and realistic end-to-end windows are:

  • Durban, South Africa — the workhorse port for Southern Africa, feeding Johannesburg by rail and road. Sea LCL/FCL door-to-door typically lands in the 25–40 day range.
  • Cape Town & Gqeberha (Port Elizabeth), South Africa — serve the south and west of the country, with slightly longer sea transit than Durban.
  • Mombasa, Kenya & Dar es Salaam, Tanzania — the main East African gateways, with inland delivery to Uganda and Rwanda adding days on top.
  • Lagos (Apapa/Tin Can), Nigeria — the biggest West African market but the most congested; budget extra time for berthing and clearance.
  • Tema, Ghana — a growing, comparatively efficient West African entry point.

Air door-to-door to South Africa typically runs 5–10 days, while air to smaller African markets can run 7–14 days depending on the onward connection. The golden rule: quote a range, and always add buffer for port congestion and local public holidays — both are more volatile in Africa than on developed lanes.

South African Customs: SARS, VAT and Import Duty

South Africa runs a single national customs authority — the South African Revenue Service (SARS) — which keeps the rules consistent whether your goods enter through Durban, Cape Town or Johannesburg's OR Tambo. The key charges on import are:

  • VAT (15%): applied to most imported goods on the customs value plus duty plus certain costs. Importers registered for VAT can generally reclaim it as an input credit.
  • Import duty: varies widely by HS code. Many consumer goods sit in the low single digits, while textiles, apparel, footwear and certain finished goods carry higher protective rates. The exact rate is driven by the HS code, not the product name.
  • Customs clearance and handling fees: a per-entry declaration fee plus any inspection, storage or demurrage if the goods are held.

Quoting DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) removes the buyer-side friction: your forwarder clears the goods, pays VAT and duty, and delivers with nothing owed at the door. For marketplace and B2C sellers this is usually the cleanest option — the customer sees one price and no surprise charges at delivery.

Documentation That Keeps Your Shipment Moving

African customs is paperwork-heavy, and a missing certificate can hold a container for weeks. Before your goods leave China, confirm you have:

  • Commercial invoice and packing list — accurate, matching the declaration, with clear HS codes and value.
  • Bill of lading or air waybill — correctly consigned to match the clearance party.
  • Certificate of origin — often required and, where a trade agreement applies, can reduce or remove duty.
  • Product-specific certificates — for South Africa, items such as electrical goods and certain regulated categories may need approval from bodies like the NRCS or SABS; textiles and some consumer goods have their own requirements.

The most reliable way to avoid delays is to run the HS codes and documents past a forwarder who clears African cargo every week — small mistakes that are cosmetic on a European lane can be expensive in Lagos or Durban.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Don't treat Africa as one country. A quote to Durban tells you nothing about the cost to reach Lagos or Kigali.
  • Don't under-declare value. African customs authorities cross-check declarations aggressively, and under-valuation attracts penalties and delays.
  • Don't ignore HS-code-specific certificates. Regulated goods need approval before departure; fixing it after arrival is slow and costly.
  • Don't book with no buffer. Port congestion and holidays are more volatile here — build slack into your promised delivery dates.
  • Don't skip a local clearance partner. On congested lanes like Lagos, a reliable local agent is the difference between days and weeks.
