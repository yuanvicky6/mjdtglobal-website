China to Bangladesh Shipping Documents & Customs Clearance 2026: Bill of Entry, PSI, HS Code and the C&F Handover

Bangladesh consignments rarely fail because the freight was wrong. They fail because one line on a commercial invoice no longer matches the Bill of Entry, or because pre-shipment inspection was booked against a proforma invoice revised a week later. This guide follows the paperwork chain from a Chinese factory to a Bangladeshi gate: which document is created at which stage, why inspection is not a formality, how classification sets the total tax incidence, and where the handover to a clearing agent decides the release date.

Published 14 November 2026 · Reading time ~9 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Documentation Is the Critical Path, Not Freight

Chattogram handles the large majority of Bangladesh's seaborne trade, and the port's own time-release studies keep reaching the same conclusion: most of the clearance clock is consumed before customs assesses anything. Importers and their clearing agents account for the bulk of elapsed time; the port, customs administration and other agencies for a much smaller share. In one recent study, machinery imports spent more than half their clearance period between the arrival manifest and the filing of the declaration. The lever is therefore not the shipping line, and not the assessment officer — it is the document set, which should be complete and internally consistent before the vessel berths.

The legal frame has also moved. The Customs Act 2023 replaced legislation in force since 1969, and it formalises electronic declaration through ASYCUDA World, risk-based channel selection and post-clearance audit. Filing on paper is no longer a fallback. A shipment that arrives with documents that cannot be filed electronically simply waits.

The Document Set, Ordered by Stage

The mistake most exporters make is treating the paperwork as one bundle produced at the end. It is really three bundles produced at three moments, and a change in the first propagates into the other two.

StageDocumentsWho produces itWhat breaks if it is wrong
Before the order is financedImport Registration Certificate, VAT/BIN registration, proforma invoice, insurance cover note, letter of credit or import contractImporter and its bank; you supply the proformaThe credit cannot be opened, so nothing ships; later amendments cost days

After the goods moveBill of lading or airway bill, import general manifest, bill of entry, value declaration, authorisation letter to the clearing agentCarrier, freight forwarder, importer, C&F agentDeclaration cannot be filed; storage and demurrage accrue from discharge

Three documents in that table carry most of the risk, and all are produced in China.

The commercial invoice

It must be consistent with the packing list, the bill of lading, the certificate of origin and the credit. Descriptions have to be specific enough to classify — "polyester knitted fabric, dyed, 150 gsm" rather than "textiles" — and the terms of sale must match reality. Where a credit is involved, the invoice value sits inside a ceiling authorised at the banking stage, so a price renegotiated afterwards creates a mismatch nobody downstream can fix.

The packing list

Practice expects a summary list and a detailed one. The detailed version carries carton dimensions, weights and piece counts — what physical examination is checked against. Vague weights are the most common cause of an examination report that contradicts the declaration, and a contradicted declaration means reassessment, not release.

The bill of lading

Consignee and notify party are not interchangeable. The consignee must be the party entitled to clear; the notify party is whoever the carrier contacts on arrival. Where the credit calls for a certificate on vessel or flag conditions, that certificate is a document in the set, not a courtesy — obtaining it after departure is slow and sometimes impossible.

Pre-Shipment Inspection Is Not a Formality

Bangladesh has required pre-shipment inspection for imports since 2000, and it applies broadly rather than to a short list of categories. The inspection covers quality, quantity and — critically — the value declared, and the resulting certificate is part of what a clearing agent must present. Without it, the consignment faces examination and delay at destination instead of a clean release.

Two habits decide whether inspection helps or hurts:

  • Inspect against the final invoice, not the proforma. A report referencing quantities or values later revised is the most common source of a stalled file.
  • Book before packing is sealed. Access to goods inside a loaded container is a negotiation, and negotiation costs days.

Book it as a shipping document with a lead time, not on the day of gate-in.

Classification Decides More Than the Duty Rate

The HS code is usually treated as a duty question. It is really three questions at once: how much tax applies, whether another agency's permit is triggered, and whether the consignment is admissible at all. A code change can move a product into a permit-controlled category, which is why classification belongs before the credit is opened rather than at the port.

Assessment is a stack, not a single rate: customs duty, any regulatory or supplementary duty the category attracts, value added tax on the duty-paid value, and advance taxes collected at the border. Each layer applies on top of the last, so the effective burden always exceeds the headline rate — and the composition changes with each annual budget.

ComponentWhat it attaches toWhy it matters to a shipper

For the importer, the practical rule is to obtain the classification position in writing — from a broker or a ruling route — and then hold every downstream document to it. For the exporter, it means the description and composition on the invoice have to be accurate enough for someone else to reach the same code independently.

Permits Now Run Through the Single Window

Certificates, licences and permits for import and export have been channelled through the Bangladesh Single Window since mid-2025, with submission made compulsory and manual permits no longer accepted. Nineteen agencies are integrated, covering standards, drugs, explosives, telecommunications, plant quarantine, livestock and economic zones — with more agencies in the pipeline, a dedicated commissionerate being set up to run the platform, and an automated risk-management system in rollout through late 2026. Traders register with their business identification number, and approved permits are referenced in the declaration.

The division of labour is frequently misunderstood. The application belongs to the importer; the evidence it depends on — formulation, test reports, specifications, model numbers — comes from the manufacturer. When a Chinese supplier cannot produce that evidence quickly, no amount of freight planning recovers the lost week.

The C&F Handover

Clearing and forwarding agents do the technical filing in practice, though an importer may legally file its own declaration. After discharge:

  1. The carrier files the import general manifest and customs approves it.
  2. The agent files the bill of entry in ASYCUDA World, then submits the printed declaration with the supporting set.
  3. The declaration is routed by risk channel — document review, or physical examination against the packing list.
  4. Assessment notice issues with the duty calculation, duties are paid electronically, delivery order and port charges are settled, and the goods are released.

Two small items stall this sequence more than anything else: an authorisation letter that does not match the importer's registered particulars, and an agent whose licence has lapsed, which causes the filing to be rejected outright.

Air freight shortens the flow — an airway bill replaces the bill of lading — but the declaration, the inspection certificate and the permits are identical. Air compresses transit, not paperwork, so a documentation gap hurts more because there is less slack to absorb it.

The clock is unforgiving in both modes. Chattogram grants a short free period on general cargo, commonly four days from discharge, after which storage charges escalate in slabs; on the duty side, unpaid amounts attract penalty interest after a set window. Both clocks start from events at the port, not from the moment anyone notices a missing certificate.

A Pre-Loading Checklist That Earns Its Keep

  • Classification settled and written down before the credit is opened, with the same description on every document.
  • Invoice, packing list, origin certificate and credit cross-checked line by line, including weights and piece counts.
  • Inspection booked against the final invoice, with goods accessible at the time of the visit.
  • Product evidence ready for any permit the importer must obtain, sent as a document pack rather than in chat.
  • Authorisation letter signed and dated, matching the importer's registered particulars.
  • Landed cost built on the total tax incidence, not on invoice value plus freight.

Common Pitfalls

  • Revising the invoice after inspection. The certificate then describes goods that no longer match the file.
  • Using a generic description. "Spare parts" cannot be classified consistently and invites examination.
  • Treating the permit as the importer's problem. The evidence behind it is almost always the manufacturer's.
  • Assuming air freight means less paperwork. It means less time to fix the same paperwork.
  • Filing late and blaming the port. Most of the clearance clock runs before the declaration is submitted.
