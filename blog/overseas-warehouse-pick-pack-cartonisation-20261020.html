Overseas Warehouse Pick & Pack 2026: Cartonisation, Packaging & Dimensional Weight Control

Inbound receives your stock and storage holds it, but nothing earns until the outbound pass runs clean. Pick and pack is where two parcels containing the same product carry different labour minutes, different carton volume and therefore different freight. This guide covers the outbound side: pick methods and slotting, cartonisation rules, dimensional weight, a written packaging specification, and how the process behaves under peak-season load.

Published 20 October 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Why Pick and Pack Sets the Cost per Parcel

When sellers review fulfilment cost they usually start with the carrier invoice, because it is the most legible line. But the invoice is largely a consequence of decisions made minutes earlier at the packing bench: which carton was available, how much void fill went in, whether the SKU was reachable without a walk, and whether dimensions were captured or estimated. Measuring only the outbound freight rate means optimising the last variable in the chain.

The cost per parcel has four visible components — pick labour, pack materials, billed shipping weight or volume, and the failure tail of damages, reships and returns. Three of the four are decided inside the warehouse. That is why the highest-return projects in fulfilment are unglamorous: replace guesswork on carton selection with a rule, replace tribal knowledge with a specification, and measure dimensional data rather than assuming it.

The Pick Path: Four Methods and Where Each Fits

Pick method shapes walking time, and walking is the largest share of pick labour on any non-automated floor. The right method depends on your order shape — lines per order, units per line, how much of your volume is single-item — not on which method sounds most advanced.

MethodHow it worksStrengthSuits
Discrete single-order pickingOne picker completes one order start to finishSimple, easy to train, easy to audit, fastest exception handlingLow volume, high-value or highly variable orders
Batch pickingOne picker collects for several orders in a single passCuts travel per unit; scales without a system overhaulMany small orders sharing popular SKUs
Zone or wave pickingFloor is split into zones; orders move zone to zone or are released in wavesHigh throughput, parallelised work, clear ownership of a zoneLarger catalogues with steady daily order flow
Cluster cart pickingCart carries multiple order totes at once into one zoneDense pick density with less sortation infrastructure than a full conveyorMid-volume operations with medium line counts

Most operations at scale use a mix: batch or cluster logic for the fast-moving single-item tail, discrete handling for multi-line and oversized orders. Ask a prospective warehouse which method applies to your order profile and how they switch as volume changes — a single fixed answer means your orders get fitted to their process.

Slotting: The Cheapest Speed You Will Ever Buy

Slotting places inventory by demand rather than convenience: fastest movers at waist height near the pack benches, items frequently bought together grouped, heavy or bulky units at floor level to cut handling risk. Review it on a cycle, because seasonality moves SKUs through velocity tiers and a static plan degrades every quarter.

Two rules hold across almost every catalogue. Slot by order frequency rather than units shipped — an accessory that appears in a third of orders belongs closer than a bulky item with similar unit volume. And separate anything visually confusable: variant mis-picks cost a reship and a return.

Cartonisation: Decide the Box Before the Box Decides You

Cartonisation is the set of rules that selects the box. Left informal, the packer takes whatever carton is nearest, the order ships in air, and the customer receives a parcel three sizes too large. Formalised, the system recommends the smallest carton that fits with defined clearance, and volume falls without anyone working faster.

The practical prerequisite is a defined carton suite rather than an open-ended cupboard: a finite set of sizes covering your order mix, including two or three mailers for soft goods, makes cost predictable and packing decisions fast. Ask how many carton SKUs are held for your account and request the list; a long, uncontrolled list means carton choice is made by habit.

Set rules for the exceptions — multi-bag splits, odd-shaped items that must ship in their own carton, gift sets, fragile goods needing protective clearance. Without explicit exception rules, either everything becomes fragile or nothing does.

Dimensional Weight: The Number Carriers Actually Bill

Domestic parcels in most markets are billed on the greater of actual weight and dimensional (volumetric) weight, derived by dividing the carton volume by a carrier divisor. The divisor is set per carrier and service and must be confirmed with them, but the consequence is universal: a light bulky parcel bills as though heavy because it occupies expensive space in a vehicle.

Lightweight categories — apparel, soft home goods, plush toys, small accessories — are structurally exposed. Two habits help. Compress where the product tolerates it, since removing a few centimetres of carton height can move a parcel down a billing tier, and capture real dimensions rather than reusing a default: a dimension scan at the bench costs seconds and prevents both under-billing that becomes a surcharge and over-billing you never see.

Finally, watch oversize and non-conveyable thresholds: crossing them adds handling charges that dwarf whatever the looser carton saved.

Packaging Materials as a Written Specification

Materials are usually bought ad hoc, then varied by whoever is packing that day. Treat them as controlled inputs — each with a defined grade and a reason — so cost stays comparable between periods and quality survives staff turnover.

ElementWhat to specifyWhy it matters
Outer cartonSize suite, board grade, closure methodGoverns dimensional weight and stacking survival
MailersWhere soft goods ship without a cartonLower volume and material cost where the product tolerates it
Void fill / dunnageType and fill standard, not "as needed"Uncontrolled fill is the most common source of excess volume
Tape and closuresWidth, grade, tamper-evident requirementSeal failure during sorting becomes a damage claim
Label and documentsPlacement surface, print quality, scan orientationUnreadable labels stop scans and stall tracking
Inner protectionBubble, moulded or none, by product tierProtects fragile or liquid contents beyond a sticker

Review the specification whenever you add a product line.

Branded and Sustainable Packaging Without Losing Throughput

Branded touches — insert cards, thank-you notes, QR codes — lift repeat purchase cheaply, but every addition is labour seconds and another item that can go missing, so put them on the written pack sheet with a defined position and sequence. Recyclable, right-sized packaging has likewise moved from preference to baseline expectation, and the commercial answer is standardisation rather than novelty: fewer materials, clearly recyclable, sized to the product. Confirm requirements per destination — recyclability standards, labelling language and producer-responsibility obligations vary and are the seller's to verify.

Packing Quality, Damage and the Return You Didn't Budget For

A "not as described" or "arrived damaged" return is a full failure of one parcel: outbound freight, return handling, inspection labour and frequently a write-off rather than a restock. Damage rates deserve a control chart, not anecdote — track them per thousand parcels by SKU and carton type, and a pattern usually resolves to one product packed too loosely or one carton grade too light.

Three habits do most of the work: drop and shake testing before a new line ships, photographing a sample of packed orders, and evidence standards high enough to support a claim. Undocumented claims are declined regardless of the carrier relationship.

Scan Discipline and the Outbound Data Trail

Scan events are the difference between "the warehouse says it shipped" and proof. Require scans at pick, at pack, at weight-and-dimension capture and at carrier manifest, with label printing reconciled to the order, not merely to the carton. Those events let you answer a support query in minutes, dispute a loss, or demonstrate delivery performance to a marketplace.

Insist the tracking number returns to your store automatically: sync gaps cause most avoidable "where is my order" tickets and cost more in support time than the integration costs to fix.

Peak Season: Where the Process Breaks

Every outbound process is stress-tested during the fourth-quarter window and major regional sale days, and the failures are predictable. Labour is the first constraint — seasonal staff know less of the catalogue, so standards need simplifying rather than more instruction. Staging space fills next, so outbound and inbound must be planned against the same floor. And the published cut-off stops being a statement and becomes a daily test of capacity in the last two hours before collection.

Plan the peak before it arrives: forecast unit and line volume, agree additional labour and overflow storage in writing, extend cut-offs only where the plan supports them, and pre-kit your top sellers so peak orders touch fewer hands. Common Pitfalls to Avoid in 2026

  • Comparing fulfilment cost on the carrier invoice alone. Carton choice created most of that number.
  • Letting carton selection stay informal. A defined carton suite and exception rules pay back quickly.
  • Ignoring dimensional weight. Light bulky goods are billed on volume, and the divisors differ by carrier — verify them.
  • Treating materials as ad hoc purchases. Unspecified dunnage is the single most common source of excess volume.
  • Planning peak from last year's totals. Labour, staging space and cut-offs fail first, and they fail on your busiest days.

Carrier divisors, surcharge thresholds and destination packaging rules change over time: confirm current terms with your carrier and compliance adviser before setting standards for a new programme.
