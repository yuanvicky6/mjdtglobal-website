China to Bangladesh EPZ & Bonded Warehouse Logistics 2026: Duty-Exempt Inputs into Export Production

A Bangladesh factory is not just an address — it sits inside a duty regime. When a plant manufactures for export, the fabric, trims, packaging and machinery it imports from China can usually enter under bond, with import duty deferred or exempted rather than paid up front. That treatment comes with conditions: a licensed facility, a bond register, rules about where the goods may go, and a reconciliation obligation at the end. Shipping into a bonded or zone facility is a different exercise from shipping into an ordinary domestic warehouse.

Published 10 October 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Why the Duty Regime, Not the Port, Sets the Plan

Two identical containers can arrive at Chittagong on the same vessel for the same factory and still follow completely different procedures. A normal domestic import attracts duty and VAT at clearance. An export-oriented manufacturer importing against a bonded or zone regime can bring the same inputs in with duty deferred or exempted — provided the goods, the documents and the movement all match the licence.

That is why the regime question belongs at the quotation stage. Before a rate is fixed, the forwarder needs to know whether the cargo moves under bond, which facility receives it, and whether it must travel on a prescribed in-bond route. Getting this wrong re-cuts the paperwork after the vessel has sailed.

The EPZ network is the most structured: zones sit near the port at Chittagong and inland in the Savar, Narayanganj, Cumilla, Ishwardi, Mongla and northern belts, so identical cargo can face a dockside delivery or a multi-day inland haul. Economic zones under BEZA are broader in scope and still expanding. Bonded warehouses, by contrast, need not sit inside any zone — the privilege attaches to the licence and the goods, not to the fence line.

What "Under Bond" Actually Means

Duty deferral is often described as if the duty disappears. It does not. Under bond, the manufacturer accepts obligations in exchange for not paying duty at import: a licensed facility, a bond register recording every input received and consumed, an input-output relationship showing how much of each input goes into each finished unit, an allowance for process wastage, an obligation to export within a defined period, and periodic reconciliation against customs records.

For the China-side supplier this means document discipline rather than a special shipping channel. The invoice and packing list must line up with what the bond covers, classification must stay consistent shipment to shipment, and quantities must be reconcilable. A bonded import does not make cargo invisible — it makes it accountable.

Which Inputs Typically Qualify

The qualifying list is set by the licence and its scheme, so the consignee's clearing agent owns the final answer. The categories that most often enter under a production bond are:

  • Raw materials and components consumed in manufacture — fabric, yarn, thread, zippers, buttons, elastic, labels, trims, electronic parts.
  • Packaging that leaves with the finished goods — cartons, polybags, hangers, hangtags, insert cards.
  • Machinery, tooling and spare parts used in the plant's own production, sometimes on a separate project basis for a new line or a new zone facility.
  • Samples and test materials for development and quality approval, usually on the fastest available lane.

What generally does not qualify is anything aimed at the domestic market: goods for local resale, consumer durables for staff or the local trade, and machinery unrelated to production. Assume nothing — a mismatch between the invoice description and the productive use the bond covers is the most common reason a bonded consignment stalls.

The DTA Moment: When Bonded Goods Go Domestic

Bangladesh distinguishes the privileged export-production world from the Domestic Tariff Area (DTA) — the ordinary domestic market. Goods imported under bond are expected to leave as exports. If bonded inputs, or goods made from them, are sold domestically instead, the deferred duty comes due at that point under the applicable scheme.

This is where loose tracking shows up. A partial domestic sale may require a fresh declaration, duty on the relevant input content and a reconciliation adjustment. Manufacturers with a clean bond register treat this as routine; those without can find a modest sale triggering a disproportionate clean-up. From the supplier's side, the lesson is to keep quantities and descriptions accurate.

Documents: Bond, LC and Customs Paperwork Must Agree

On this lane the paperwork is the shipment. Import payments often run through letters of credit — frequently back-to-back, with the factory's export LC underpinning the import LC for its inputs — and the bond adds a second layer of matching on top.

  1. Bond or licence references clearly identified so the consignment is recognised as privileged cargo.
  2. Contract or LC linkage connecting the input shipment to the export order it feeds.
  3. Commercial invoice whose description matches the bond coverage, with a stable HS classification.
  4. Packing list with accurate weights and quantities — this is what the bond register is reconciled against.
  5. In-bond movement documents where the goods travel under bond between the port and the facility.
  6. ISPM 15 marking on all wood packaging; untreated crates and pallets are rejected on sight.
  7. Dangerous goods documentation for chemicals, with correct classification and marking arranged at origin.

Machinery, Spares and Samples Move Differently

Treat the three cargo types as three separate plans. Machinery is a project: heavy, often needing cranage or a planned unloading window, and frequently tied to a new line or zone build where the exemption basis sits apart from routine inputs. Spares are the emergency lane — when a machine is down, the part flies. Samples are the clock lane, where development rounds need days and a reliable pickup-to-factory pipeline wins repeat business far beyond the freight spend. Pre-Loading Checklist for Bonded and Zone Cargo

  1. Confirm the regime first. Bonded, zone-based or ordinary import — before anything is quoted.
  2. Identify the receiving facility. EPZ, economic zone or a licensed bonded warehouse, and its distance from the port.
  3. Verify bond coverage. Confirm the goods fall within the licence and the applicable scheme.
  4. Align documents across three systems. Bond reference, LC terms and customs description must agree.
  5. Check the permitted movement. Establish whether the inland leg must follow a prescribed in-bond route with specific transit documents.
  6. Plan the inland leg explicitly. Trucking into the factory belt is days of lead time, not a footnote.
  7. Flag wood packaging and dangerous goods. ISPM 15 marking and chemical documentation are origin-side decisions.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid in 2026

  • Deciding the regime after booking. It changes the document set, the route and the cost.
  • Assuming "under bond" means duty-free forever. Deferral turns into a liability the moment goods go domestic.
  • Loose descriptions. An invoice that does not match the bond coverage is the most common cause of a stalled consignment.
  • Forgetting that the inland leg is real. Port-to-factory trucking eats schedules; build in buffer.
  • Skipping ISPM 15 on crates and pallets. Untreated wood is refused outright.
  • Treating machinery like ordinary cargo. Projects need unloading and installation windows planned at origin.

Bangladesh's zone framework and bonded procedures are revised periodically, and individual privileges depend on the licence held by the consignee. Treat this guide as directional and confirm the current position with your counterparty.
