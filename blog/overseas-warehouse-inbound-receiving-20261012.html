Overseas Warehouse Inbound Receiving 2026: ASN Data, Discrepancy Handling & Inventory Accuracy

Sellers obsess over outbound — pick, pack, ship — and treat inbound as a formality the supplier and the warehouse will sort out between themselves. Then the first shipment lands, the receiving report shows a short count, two cartons are unlabelled, and the seller is arguing about money that has already left China. Inbound receiving is the handoff where your landed cost, inventory accuracy and dispute position are fixed at once. This guide covers the 2026 discipline for the supplier-to-warehouse handoff.

Published 12 October 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Why Inbound Receiving Is Where Money Leaks

An overseas warehouse only becomes an asset when the stock inside it is verified, labelled and searchable. Until then it is a black box: goods physically present but commercially unusable, because the system cannot promise a SKU, a quantity or a condition. The deeper problem is that receiving happens far from the seller, at the moment when the person who packed the goods is already gone. Your only leverage then is the documentation prepared before the shipment left China — good inbound discipline is really pre-shipment discipline, tested at the destination dock.

The ASN: The One File That Prevents Everything Else

The advance shipping notice (ASN) is the single most useful document in the inbound chain. It is not a label bolted onto an email; it is a structured data file that tells the warehouse exactly what should arrive, in what cartons, in what quantities, so receiving becomes verification rather than guesswork. A warehouse holding your ASN before arrival can pre-build the receipt, schedule labour and flag special handling. A workable ASN carries the ASN and PO/SO references, the carrier reference and expected arrival window, the carton count with per-carton contents by SKU and quantity plus dimensions and weight, the labelling format, any handling flags such as batteries, and the declared value and origin for customs. Send it when the shipment is booked — not after it sails.

Carton Labelling and SSCC: A Shenzhen Decision

Labels are printed at origin, so the labelling decision is made in Shenzhen, not at the destination dock. A carton that arrives with no label or an unreadable barcode will be quarantined and re-labelled by hand, and billed for handling — often received as unknown stock first. Two habits remove most of the pain. Give every carton a machine-readable identifier, ideally an SSCC barcode, and make sure the same identifier appears in the ASN so scanning the box matches it to the file instantly. Then make SKU labels durable and unambiguous — a scannable unit barcode plus a human-readable SKU and quantity on the outer carton — so a manual check is possible even when the scanner fails. Confirm your 3PL's exact label specification before the first order is packed; format expectations differ between warehouses and marketplace prep rules.

The Receiving Flow, Step by Step

Once the goods are at the dock, a well-run process moves through a predictable sequence:

  1. Dock appointment and arrival. The warehouse slots the delivery, checks carrier paperwork and confirms the shipment against the ASN.
  2. Unload and count. Cartons are counted at the door and checked for visible damage before entering the racks.
  3. Scan and match. Each carton identifier is scanned against the ASN line; mismatches are flagged while the carrier is still on site.
  4. Condition inspection. Seals, carton integrity and handling flags are checked; damaged cartons are photographed before opening.
  5. Put-away. Verified stock is assigned to bin locations and the inventory system is updated.
  6. Quarantine for exceptions. Unlabelled, damaged or unknown stock is held separately, never mixed into sellable inventory.
  7. Receiving report. The warehouse issues what was received, by SKU and condition, against the ASN.

The critical detail is step three. Discrepancies noted while the carrier is still at the dock are backed by a signed delivery record; discrepancies found days later are far harder to attribute.

Carton Receiving vs Unit Receiving

Not all inbound needs the same depth of checking. The trade-off between speed and certainty changes with the product and the carton structure:

AspectCarton-level receivingUnit-level receiving
SpeedFast — whole cartons scanned and counted at the doorSlower — every unit handled and scanned
What is verifiedCarton count against the ASN; contents trusted to the manifestEach unit against the manifest
Best suited toSealed supplier cartons, high-volume, low-variance SKUsMixed cartons, high-value goods, regulated categories
Cost exposureLower handling charge, but a wrong manifest propagates silentlyHigher handling charge, but errors surface at the door
Main riskStock counted as present that is not actually correctThroughput slows and labour cost rises

Most sellers run a hybrid: carton-level for repeat, stable SKUs, and unit-level for a new SKU's first shipment, for high-value or regulated goods, and as a periodic audit sample on mature lines.

Discrepancy Types and How to Resolve Them

Discrepancies are normal. What separates a resilient operation is a defined response for each type before it appears:

DiscrepancyTypical causeResolution path
Short shipmentSupplier short-packed, or the load was splitConfirm against ASN and carrier documents; file a carrier or supplier claim; amend the PO
Over shipmentDuplicate loading or an extra palletHold in quarantine as unknown stock; await instruction before any put-away
Wrong or mixed SKUMislabel at the factory or mixed production runsRe-label or quarantine; correct the SKU mapping in the warehouse system
Damage in transitWeak packaging, rough handling or a wet containerPhotograph before opening; file a cargo claim inside the carrier's notice window
Unlabelled stockLabels missing, illegible or unreadable barcodesRe-label or quarantine; correct the origin process

Two rules keep this manageable: quarantine should carry a review deadline, because aged exceptions quietly become write-offs; and every discrepancy has a notice window on the carrier or supplier side, so the paper trail assembled at the dock is what makes a later claim possible.

Inventory-Accuracy Targets That Keep the Warehouse Honest

Inventory accuracy is not a feeling; it is a measured ratio of system stock to physical stock, reviewed SKU by SKU. A warehouse reporting high headline accuracy can still be wrong precisely on the SKUs that matter, so ask for accuracy by SKU tier rather than one blended figure — high-value and fast-moving lines deserve a tighter standard than slow-moving, low-value items. The discipline behind the number matters more than the target: cycle counting a rotating slice of SKUs catches drift before it compounds, root-cause review stops the same error recurring, and a shared view of on-hand, on-order and in-transit stock means you never replenish on numbers the warehouse has already superseded. Falling accuracy is a receiving or put-away problem and will eventually surface as oversells and refunds on the storefront.

Reconciling With the 3PL: Claims Windows and Data Feeds

Inbound quality is set as much by commercial terms as by the physical process, and two clauses deserve attention before any goods move. The first is the claims and discrepancy window — how long after receipt you have to dispute a discrepancy, and what evidence the warehouse will supply. Windows that are short, or that start at arrival rather than receipt, quietly transfer risk to the seller. The second is the data agreement: what inventory feeds you receive, how often, and in what format, so your records and the warehouse's can be reconciled without manual cross-checking. Most disputes that stall do so on documentation, not intent; the deciding factor is whether the ASN, carrier record, receiving report and photographs all agree.

First-Time Onboarding vs Steady-State Restocking

The first inbound into a new warehouse should never look like the tenth. The initial shipment is a calibration exercise: send a controlled, well-labelled quantity, insist on unit-level verification, and reconcile it line by line against your records before scaling. This tests whether the ASN format is understood, whether the labels scan, whether the receiving report is usable and whether discrepancies surface promptly — all far cheaper to fix on one small shipment than on a container. Once the lane is proven, steady-state restocking can run carton-level with recurring ASN formats and periodic audits, so each inbound becomes a routine confirmation rather than a task needing your personal attention.

Common Pitfalls

  • Shipping before the ASN is ready. The warehouse cannot verify what it was never told to expect.
  • Leaving labelling to the destination. Re-labelling at the dock is the most expensive way to fix a Shenzhen decision.
  • Counting after the driver leaves. Discrepancies lose their paper trail the moment the carrier departs.
  • Letting quarantine become long-term storage. Aged exceptions quietly turn into write-offs.
  • Accepting one blended accuracy figure. Ask for accuracy by SKU tier, not a headline average.
  • Scaling before the first shipment is reconciled. The onboarding shipment is a test, not a shortcut.
