Europe DDP Clearance Gateways 2026: Rotterdam vs Antwerp vs Hamburg vs Gdansk & the Poland Rail Corridor

Most sellers treat Europe DDP as a single product with a single price. In practice, the all-in rate is largely decided by one upstream choice: which gateway your cargo enters the EU through. Rotterdam, Antwerp, Hamburg, Gdansk and the Poland rail corridor each carry different inland transport cost, clearance throughput, documentation friction and VAT mechanics. This guide compares the main options and shows how gateway selection — not the ocean freight rate alone — drives your landed cost per unit.

Published 21 September 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Why the Entry Gateway Is a Landed-Cost Decision

Under 欧洲清提派 (clearance–pickup–delivery, the Chinese term for door-to-door DDP), the forwarder controls the whole chain: origin pickup in China, main carriage, EU import clearance, duty and VAT settlement, and inland delivery to the consignee or an Amazon fulfilment centre. Only the first links of that chain are fixed when you book; the gateway you enter through determines the rest.

Four factors move with the gateway:

  • Inland distance to the destination. A container discharged in Gdansk and delivered to Warsaw is far cheaper inland than the same container discharged in Rotterdam.
  • Clearance throughput. Large gateways process more declarations per day and shorten dwell time, but inspection profiles differ by port and member state.
  • VAT mechanics. Whether import VAT is paid at release or deferred depends on the country of entry and the importer's status there.
  • Documentation load. Filing obligations and any onward EU movement change depending on whether clearance and delivery happen in the same country.

Two quotes for identical cargo can therefore differ meaningfully in total cost while using the same ocean rate.

Gateway-by-Gateway: Strengths and Practical Notes

Rotterdam and Antwerp

Rotterdam and Antwerp are the default choice for sellers distributing across multiple EU countries, and for Amazon FBA inbound covering several marketplaces under one contract. Both offer very high declaration throughput, mature bonded infrastructure, and dense daily feeder connections from Chinese ports. The trade-off is purely geographic: for a final destination in Poland, Hungary or Austria, the inland leg is long and expensive, and the truck leg becomes a much larger share of the all-in rate.

Hamburg and Bremerhaven

Hamburg is the natural entry point when the delivery country is Germany itself — the largest single EU e-commerce market and the usual home of a seller's German VAT registration. Clearance and delivery in the same country simplifies the DDP chain considerably: no onward intra-EU movement to track, no second-country registration, and a shorter inland leg to German fulfilment centres and 3PL warehouses.

Gdansk and the Polish Baltic gateways

Gdansk has become a meaningful alternative for Central European destinations. Inland distance to Warsaw, Prague, Katowice or Budapest is short relative to a Rotterdam discharge, and Polish warehouses are widely used for EU-wide FBA prep. The constraint is service choice: fewer direct China–Gdansk strings than China–Rotterdam, so scheduling flexibility is narrower and cut-off discipline matters more.

The Poland rail corridor

For cargo that cannot wait for a sea transit but does not justify air freight, the China–Poland rail route remains a practical middle option. Goods cross at the Poland–Belarus border crossing complex, change gauge, and move onward by rail or truck to Central European hubs. Rail sits between sea and air on both cost and transit time, and suits replenishment cycles of a few weeks rather than urgent restocks. Note that rail capacity is finite and seasonal peaks can tighten space well in advance.

Southern European gateways

Trieste, Koper, Barcelona and Valencia make sense only when the destination is genuinely in southern Europe. Shipping to a Spanish or Italian fulfilment centre via Rotterdam adds an unnecessary inland leg across the continent; a Mediterranean discharge is both cheaper inland and faster to the final door. The reverse is equally true — routing northern European cargo through the Mediterranean is almost always the wrong trade.

What ICS2 Advance Filing Changed

The EU's Import Control System 2 (ICS2) has progressively extended advance electronic security filing to more transport modes: air cargo first, then maritime, road and rail. The practical effect for sellers is simple — the data supplied at booking time must now be complete and accurate well before departure, not at arrival.

  • Complete consignee data. Placeholder addresses and missing contact details are a common cause of filing rejection.
  • Accurate goods description. Generic entries such as "gift" or "accessories" are frequently questioned; a plain-language description with a correct HS code is required.
  • Consistency across documents. Invoice, packing list, bill of lading and filing must agree on values, weights and parties.

A competent DDP forwarder files on your behalf, but only if the underlying commercial data is right. Data quality is now a departure-readiness issue, not an arrival issue.

Import VAT: Pay at Release or Defer?

Import VAT is charged when goods are released for free circulation. Whether it must be paid over at that moment or can be deferred depends on the country of import and the importer's status there. The Netherlands and Belgium are well known for postponed import VAT accounting for registered importers; Germany and Poland operate deferment arrangements subject to authorisation and guarantees.

On a DDP shipment the forwarder settles the import, so the mechanism affects cash flow and the guarantee behind the quote rather than what the buyer sees. Sellers should still ask which country clears the import and under whose VAT number, since import VAT paid in one member state is not automatically recoverable in another.

A Simple Gateway Selection Checklist

  1. Name the final destination first. Postcode or fulfilment centre, not just "Europe".
  2. Keep clearance and delivery in the same country if possible. Fewer documents, less risk, shorter inland legs.
  3. Match mode to the restock cycle. Sea for planned volume, rail for medium urgency, air for launch quantities.
  4. Confirm the VAT mechanism before booking. Ask where the import clears, under whose number, and whether deferment applies.
  5. Screen commodity-specific requirements early. Product regulation, labelling and packaging rules should be checked before departure.
  6. Compare all-in, not freight-only. The cheapest ocean leg is meaningless if the inland leg from that gateway is the longest on the continent.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid in 2026

  • Choosing a gateway on ocean freight alone. The inland leg from a distant port frequently erases the saving.
  • Clearing in one country and delivering in another without planning it. Cross-border delivery after clearance needs its own documentation.
  • Sending incomplete booking data. A missing consignee address or vague goods description can stop a shipment before it is loaded.
  • Assuming import VAT treatment is identical across the EU. Deferment is country-specific and conditions apply.
  • Treating the UK as part of the EU chain. The UK has its own customs, VAT and filing regime.
  • Locking in routing before the destination is fixed. The optimal gateway often changes with the final address.
