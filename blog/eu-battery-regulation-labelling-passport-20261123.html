EU Battery Regulation 2026: Labelling, Battery Passports & the February 2027 Wall for China Battery Exports

If you ship battery-powered goods from China into Europe — power tools, e-bikes, consumer electronics, storage packs or the batteries themselves — Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 now decides as much about your clearance and go-to-market as the customs tariff does. Several obligations already bind importers today, one headline labelling date turned out to be conditional, and a hard wall of QR codes, battery passports and removability requirements arrives on 18 February 2027. This guide separates what applies now from what is still pending, and explains what each layer demands from a DDP arrangement.

Published 23 November 2026 · Reading time ~7 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

What the Battery Regulation Actually Is

Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 replaced the old Batteries Directive 2006/66/EC and has applied since 18 February 2024. The word choice matters: as a regulation it applies directly in every member state, with no national transposition to soften or delay it. It defines five battery categories — portable (up to 5 kg), light means of transport or LMT (up to 25 kg), SLI, industrial and EV — and it follows the battery wherever it travels, including batteries incorporated into products.

Two consequences matter for Chinese supply chains. First, conformity assessment and CE marking obligations for batteries began applying on 18 August 2024, so CE-marked products containing unmarked batteries are a compliance mismatch that market surveillance authorities increasingly notice. Second, because the regulation also applies in Northern Ireland under the Windsor Framework, goods routed through the UK into the island of Ireland are not automatically outside its reach.

The 18 August 2026 Labelling Date Has a Condition Built Into It

Most summaries state that from 18 August 2026 batteries must carry the general information label: manufacturer identity, battery category and identifier, place and date of manufacture, weight, capacity, chemistry, hazardous substances, the appropriate extinguishing agent and critical raw materials above 0.1% by weight. Article 13 actually says something narrower. Each of those labelling obligations applies from 18 August 2026 or eighteen months after the entry into force of the implementing act setting the harmonised labelling specifications, whichever is the later.

That implementing act was due by August 2025 but remained in draft into 2026, with adoption still pending in late 2026. Under the plain wording of the regulation, the later date governs — so the information-label obligations were not yet enforceable in late 2026 even though the nominal date had passed. Two cautions follow. The obligations that carry no condition — the crossed-out collection symbol, the Cd and Pb chemical symbols above 0.002% and 0.004% respectively — do already bind, and are the markings most Chinese factories routinely omit. And the conditional label work does not get easier for being deferred: the eighteen-month clock starts the day the act enters into force, so a compliance programme built now simply absorbs the final specifications later.

QR Codes and the Battery Passport: 18 February 2027

Two obligations arrive on the same day and are frequently confused. From 18 February 2027, every battery placed on the EU market must carry a QR code. For batteries in scope of the passport, that QR code resolves to the battery passport; for everything else it gives access to label information, the declaration of conformity and, where applicable, the carbon footprint declaration.

The passport itself applies to LMT batteries, rechargeable industrial batteries above 2 kWh and EV batteries. Three features make it more demanding than a data sheet. It is unique to each individual battery, not each model — a production run of ten thousand packs means ten thousand passports, keyed to serialised batteries. It must be registered in the EU's digital product passport register, with the operator's identity verified. And access is tiered: some fields public, some reserved for market surveillance authorities and notified bodies, some for repairers and recyclers.

The Commission's Digital Battery Passport Guide published in 2026 mapped roughly seventy-one data points across identity and traceability, carbon footprint, recycled-content shares, electrochemical performance, state-of-health parameters and dismantling and safety information. The obligation to keep the passport accurate sits with the economic operator placing the battery on the market — in a DDP chain, whoever is named importer of record. Chinese suppliers therefore need the underlying data — manufacturing plant coordinates, chemistry, recycled shares, due diligence reporting — contracted out of their own upstream tier, not assembled after the goods have sailed.

Removability: The Design Change That Catches Consumer Electronics

From 18 February 2027, portable batteries incorporated into products must be removable and replaceable by the end user, without special tools unless a tool is supplied free of charge, over the product's useful life. LMT batteries must at least be removable and replaceable by independent professionals. This is a product-design rule with border consequences: devices built around glued-in, soldered or proprietary-fastener batteries cannot be fixed by a labelling retrofit, and such goods face market-surveillance action and, in several member states, distance-selling delisting risk.

Due Diligence: The Stopped Clock Until August 2027

The regulation's supply chain due diligence obligations — covering cobalt, natural graphite, lithium and nickel — were to apply from 18 August 2025. Under Regulation (EU) 2025/1561, the "stop-the-clock" measure in the Omnibus IV simplification package adopted on 18 July 2025, application was postponed by two years to 18 August 2027. Obliged operators above the turnover threshold must adopt a due diligence policy, have it verified and periodically audited by a notified third-party body, and report publicly on it.

The postponement reflects the slow appointment of verification bodies, not a withdrawal of substance. Commission guidelines were due by July 2026; the gap is best treated as preparation time, since supplier mapping and raw-material-origin clauses take longer than a year in practice.

Carbon Footprint Declarations, In Stages

Carbon footprint declarations under Article 7 apply in stages: EV batteries first, rechargeable industrial batteries above 2 kWh next, then LMT batteries — with the EV and industrial stages tied to delegated acts on calculation methodology. Declarations are per battery model and per manufacturing plant, expressed in CO2e per kWh of stored energy, lodged with the Commission's economic operator information system. Ordinary portable batteries inside consumer products are not in this layer at all, which is why a power-bank shipment and an energy-storage container face very different files.

EPR: The Registration Layer That Already Applies

Since 18 August 2025, whoever places batteries or battery-incorporating products on an EU market is a "producer" with extended producer responsibility. That means registration in the producer register of each member state where goods are sold — registration numbers do not transfer across borders — plus financing of collection and waste treatment. Non-EU producers act through an authorised representative established in the EU. Where a DDP seller or its appointed entity is the importer of record, that entity is the producer; where goods flow onward through platforms or distributors, who holds producer status must be settled in writing, because several member states treat facilitating platforms as producers in their own right. Separately, lithium batteries remain dangerous goods for transport (UN 3480/UN 3481), constraining packing, quantities and carrier acceptance before any label question arises.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Treating 18 August 2026 as settled either way. The information-label date is conditional on a pending implementing act; the unconditional markings already bind.
  • Confusing the QR code with the passport. Every battery needs a QR code; only LMT, industrial above 2 kWh and EV batteries need a passport behind it.
  • Designing 2027 products around non-removable portable batteries. Removability is a design gate, not a labelling task.
  • Reading the due diligence delay as cancellation. The obligation moved to August 2027; the supplier mapping cannot start too early.
  • Assuming one EPR registration covers the EU. Registration is per member state, with an authorised representative for non-EU producers.
  • Leaving passport data to the forwarder. The data lives in the Chinese supply chain; the logistics partner can carry the file but cannot create it.
