EU DDP Shipping 2026: The €3 Duty, the €2 Handling Fee and IOSS for China-to-EU Parcels

The €150 duty exemption that parcel-direct sellers built their margins on is gone. Since 1 July 2026 every low-value consignment pays duty per item, and since 1 November 2026 it also carries a €2 handling fee and three product identifiers per SKU. A DDP quote for Europe is no longer a freight rate plus a percentage — it is an item-by-item calculation, and the difference between a profitable parcel and a loss now sits in your HS lines.

Published 5 November 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

What Changed, and When

The reform arrived in stages, which is why so many quotes still rest on last year's assumptions. The dates that matter:

EffectiveMeasurePractical effect
1 July 2026€150 customs duty exemption removed; temporary flat duty introduced for consignments up to €150Duty is charged per item at a flat rate instead of being waived; the flat duty is scheduled to run until 1 July 2028, after which normal tariff rates apply
1 July 2026B2C identification and declaration data tightenedEvery consignment needs buyer type, complete descriptions, HS codes and origin; simplified declaration routes are restricted for non-IOSS B2C traffic
1 November 2026Union handling fee of €2 per item; product identifiers mandatoryAn additional non-refundable charge on every distance sale, at any value, plus three identifier codes per SKU
1 July 2028EU Customs Data Hub for e-commerceFull tariff treatment replaces the temporary flat duty; the data burden becomes permanent

Read the table as a cost structure, not a news item: each row adds a line to your landed cost, and every line is calculated per item rather than per parcel.

“Per Item” Is the Whole Story

An item is not a parcel and not a unit. It is a declaration line defined by tariff classification, description and origin: two identical phone cases of the same HS code collapse into one item, while a case, a cable and a charger in the same box are three. That single definition decides your duty bill.

Take an illustrative €40 consignment of three different SKUs. Duty is 3 × €3 = €9 and the handling fee is 3 × €2 = €6, so €15 of charges land on a parcel worth €40 before VAT is applied — and VAT is charged on the duty as well as the goods. Ship the same three products as a single bundled SKU under one classification and the charges halve, though only if the bundle genuinely is one product and is described as such. What is no longer possible is the old trick of splitting orders into separate low-value parcels to stay under a threshold: there is no threshold left to stay under, and each parcel now repeats the per-item charge.

The Union Handling Fee: Small, Fixed and Never Refunded

The €2 fee is easy to dismiss on a €200 order and dangerous on an €8 one. Three properties make it a margin issue rather than an administrative one:

  • It applies at every value. Unlike the flat duty, the handling fee is not limited to consignments under €150. Above that value you pay normal ad valorem duty plus the fee.
  • It is charged per item, not per parcel. A five-line declaration for a single customer order pays €10 in fees alone.
  • It is not refundable. Return the goods and the fee stays with the customs authority. The flat €3 duty is also non-refundable on low-value returns, while VAT refunds continue to follow normal rules.

That last point quietly rewrites EU returns policy. A free-returns promise made in 2025 assumed recoverable tax; in 2026 the duty and fee on a returned low-value parcel are sunk cost, and a seller absorbing them is funding the return twice. Either restrict free returns to higher-value orders or price the expected loss into the product.

Product Identifiers: The Data Layer That Now Gates Clearance

Since 1 November 2026, consignments up to €150 must declare three identifiers per item, each under its own code in the supporting document section of the declaration:

  • M-PID (C127) — the merchant's own identifier: SKU, listing ID or variant ID, assigned by the seller or platform.
  • NS-PID (C128) — the manufacturer's non-standardised identifier: model, part or style number.
  • S-PID (C129) — a standardised identifier such as GTIN, EAN, UPC or ISBN, where one exists; otherwise the exemption statement Y081 is declared instead.

The identifiers must point to a single product variant, not a batch or a unit, and a bundle line needs the identifiers of every product inside it. Personalised, print-on-demand and refurbished goods are not exempt, and a manufacturer with no internal product number is expected to create one. In practice this is a sourcing task, not a brokerage task: the data has to be collected from factories in China alongside the packing list, because a broker at the border cannot invent a manufacturer part number when the truck is already at the gate.

IOSS or Not: Who Is the Declarant

IOSS remains the dividing line in EU parcel logistics, and the 2026 rules sharpen it. Under IOSS the seller — or the platform — collects VAT at checkout, is treated as the deemed importer, and files a single monthly return; the courier clears the goods on its behalf. Outside IOSS the picture is more fragmented.

AspectWith IOSSWithout IOSS
Who is the declarantThe IOSS holder as deemed importer, represented by the carrierThe importer of record, or the consumer in member states offering a self-declaration route
Where goods clearEntry can be centralised across the EUClearance must take place in the destination member state

VAT baseVAT collected at sale on the goodsDuty is added to the VAT base, compounding the charges

For a Chinese seller shipping direct, the practical consequence is that non-IOSS traffic now needs a clearance point in the country where the customer lives, not at whichever gateway the flight lands. That rerouting is often the real cause of the delays sellers attribute to “new customs rules”.

What This Means for DDP and DAP

DDP means the seller carries duty, the handling fee and VAT, and the buyer receives a parcel with nothing to pay. DAP means the buyer pays on delivery — and under per-item charging, the amount they are asked for at the door is larger, more visible and harder to explain than it used to be. Refused deliveries and one-star reviews follow the invoice.

So DDP has become the default for marketplace and independent-store traffic into the EU. The risk moves to the seller, and it moves into the quote itself: a credible DDP quote now has to state whether it covers the flat duty, the handling fee and VAT, and on how many items per consignment. A headline rate without those three answers is not a quote, it is a starting point for a dispute. Where a forwarder bills DDP but excludes the handling fee — a common gap this quarter, because the fee is newer than most rate cards — the seller discovers the hole only in the monthly reconciliation.

Four Levers That Still Work

  • Reduce declaration lines, honestly. Fewer items per parcel means fewer €3 and €2 charges. Combine variants into genuine multi-packs, consolidate shipments to one address, and revisit whether every accessory needs to be a separate SKU. What you cannot do is merge unrelated products under one code to save €5.
  • Move repeat volume off parcel direct. A consolidated commercial import is assessed under the normal tariff rather than the flat per-item charge, so for low-value, high-volume products the arithmetic usually favours shipping in bulk and fulfilling locally. For a €12 item, €5 of per-item charges is a structural disadvantage no carrier discount can fix.
  • Price the charge, not the freight. Build duty, fee and VAT into the shelf price at SKU level rather than averaging across the catalogue, and review any product below roughly €20 for viability once €5 of per-item charges and VAT are applied.
  • Treat the fee as sunk on returns. Decide the refund policy on landed cost, and route returns to a local hub or a consolidation point instead of paying duty twice on a re-shipment.

Parcel Direct or Bulk DDP Into an EU Warehouse?

The comparison has shifted. Parcel direct still wins on speed to first sale and on inventory risk; bulk DDP wins on unit economics the moment a SKU sells repeatedly.

The practical pattern most of our clients now run is a hybrid: bulk DDP into an EU warehouse for the top sellers, parcel direct for the tail, with the split reviewed monthly against actual per-item charges rather than a yearly freight negotiation.

Common Pitfalls

  • Quoting DDP on freight plus a duty percentage. The flat duty and handling fee are absolute amounts, not percentages; a percentage model under-recovers on cheap items and over-recovers on expensive ones.
  • Assuming the fee is limited to low-value parcels. It applies at any value, including consignments above €150 that pay normal duty.
  • Leaving product identifiers to the broker. Identifiers come from the manufacturer and the seller, not from customs.
  • Splitting orders to stay under a threshold. There is no threshold; splitting now multiplies per-item charges.
  • Promising free returns on low-value items. Duty and the handling fee are non-refundable on return.
  • Clearing non-IOSS B2C cargo at the first entry point. Low-value non-IOSS consignments must clear in the destination country.
