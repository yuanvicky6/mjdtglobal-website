China to Bangladesh Textile & RMG Supply Chain Shipping 2026: Fabric, Trims & Machinery

Bangladesh is one of the world's largest garment exporters, and the industry that produces those garments runs on imported inputs: fabric woven or knitted in China, zippers and buttons from Zhejiang, dyes and finishing chemicals, sewing machines and the spare parts that keep them running. This supply chain lane is different from consumer e-commerce — the consignees are factories, the payments move through letters of credit, and a delayed trim shipment can idle a production line. This guide explains how the China-to-Bangladesh textile and RMG supply chain lane works in 2026: what moves, by which mode, under what documents, and where the costly mistakes happen.

Published 2 October 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Why the RMG Lane Is a Supply Chain, Not a Shipping Lane

A consumer parcel arrives at a doorstep and the transaction ends. A fabric shipment arrives at a garment factory and the transaction is only beginning: the fabric is cut, sewn, finished, packed and re-exported as finished garments. Every day the input sits in transit or in customs is a day the factory's export deadline moves. Buyers on this lane therefore think in production windows, not shipping dates.

That reframing changes what "good logistics" means. The cheapest freight quote is worthless if the cargo misses a cutting schedule; the fastest option is wasted if the letter of credit documents do not match. Reliability and document discipline beat headline rates on the RMG lane.

Sea Is the Backbone: Chittagong and the Factory Leg

Bulk fabric and machinery move by sea from Shenzhen, Ningbo, Shanghai or Qingdao into Chittagong, with realistic port-to-port transit in the range of two to four weeks depending on whether the service transships through Singapore or Colombo. From Chittagong the cargo trucks to the factory clusters around Dhaka, Gazipur, Narayanganj and Savar — an inland leg that adds several days and is where port congestion and chassis shortages surface.

Two disciplines keep the sea leg predictable. First, book around the monsoon and Eid windows when yard congestion peaks. Second, pre-file the import declaration so the container does not sit through a documentation cycle at the port. Air for the Clock: Samples, Trims and Season Saves

Air freight earns its premium in three scenarios on this lane: sample and approval movement, a late trim shipment holding up a full production line, and a dead-season save where the buyer's shipment window closes. Direct flights from Shenzhen and Guangzhou into Dhaka move in days rather than weeks, and for a carton of zippers holding up a container-load of finished garments, the arithmetic is obvious.

The classic mistake is treating air as the plan rather than the exception. If a style's trims routinely need air rescue, the problem is the replenishment calendar, not the mode. See the Bangladesh air freight guide for transit windows and cost structure.

Backward Linkage: Bonded Inputs and Export Production

Bangladesh operates import-duty exemption and drawback arrangements for inputs that go directly into export production, so factories importing fabric and trims against export orders often do not pay the full import-duty stack that a consumer-goods importer would. The schemes are administered through the customs and bond authorities, and eligibility depends on the factory's status and the documentation trail.

What this means for a Chinese supplier is practical: confirm with your Bangladesh consignee before shipping whether the cargo clears under a bonded or exempted arrangement or as a normal dutiable import, because the document set differs. Shipping against the wrong assumption means either paying duty twice or watching the cargo wait while paperwork is re-cut. A licensed clearing agent at the Bangladesh end should make this call at booking time, not after arrival.

Rules of Origin: Why the Fabric Choice Matters

Bangladesh's garment exports enter the EU, UK and other markets under preferential arrangements, and those preferences depend on rules of origin — which define how much transformation must happen in Bangladesh for the garment to qualify. Depending on the destination and the applicable rule, the origin of the fabric and the documentary evidence of that origin can decide whether the buyer saves duty or pays full rates.

The logistics consequence is simple but easy to miss: keep a clean origin paper trail for every fabric and trim lot you ship. Certificates of origin, mill declarations and commercial invoices that are consistent with each other are what allow the Bangladesh factory to claim preference downstream. Sloppy origin documents do not stop your shipment — they quietly cost your customer money weeks later, which is its own kind of relationship damage. Confirm the currently applicable rule with your buyer or their customs broker, as origin regimes are periodically revised.

Documents: LC-Driven Trade Has Zero Tolerance

Import payments in Bangladesh run through the banking system under foreign-exchange controls, and most factory imports are financed by letters of credit — often back-to-back LCs, where the factory's own export LC supports the import LC for its inputs. LC documentation is unforgiving: the bill of lading, commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin and any required inspection certificate must match the LC terms exactly, or the bank refuses the documents.

  1. Match the LC word for word. Consignee name, notify party, Incoterm, port names and cargo description must mirror the LC text.
  2. Present within the deadline. LCs carry document-presentation windows; a late document set is a refused one.
  3. Certificates of origin: issue through the authorised channel, with consistent consignee and goods descriptions.
  4. Dangerous goods: chemicals need MSDS, correct UN classification and compliant marking before booking, not after.

A forwarder that understands LC shipments will check the draft documents against the LC before the vessel sails. That single habit prevents the majority of payment disputes on this lane.

The Sample Loop and the Backhaul

Garment development is iterative: a proto sample, a fit sample, a pre-production sample, each round needing days not weeks. Suppliers who build a reliable sample pipeline — pickup same day, direct flight, door delivery to the factory's merchandiser — win repeat business disproportionately to the freight spend involved.

On the rate side, remember the lane is two-directional. Bangladesh exports finished garments in huge volume; China-bound backhaul capacity exists and rates on the import leg improve when your forwarder balances both directions. A partner running both flows can price the China → Bangladesh leg sharper than one hauling empty backhaul.

Common Pitfalls on the RMG Lane

  • Booking sea freight into a production window with no buffer. Chittagong congestion and the Dhaka trucking leg eat schedules; plan weeks, not days, of margin.
  • Documents that do not match the LC. The bank, not customs, rejects these — and payment is what stalls.
  • Assuming the factory's duty treatment. Bonded or dutiable changes the document set; confirm at booking.
  • Air freighting packaging and hangers. Bulk, low-value items belong on the ocean, always.
  • Skipping ISPM 15 on pallets and crates. Untreated wood is rejected at Chittagong on sight.
  • Losing the origin paper trail. It does not block your shipment — it quietly costs your buyer their duty preference.
