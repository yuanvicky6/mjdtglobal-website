China to Australia Sea Freight 2026: Port Choice & the Landside Cost Stack

Ocean rates from China to Australia sit inside a fairly narrow band, whichever gateway you nominate. The invoice that separates an efficient shipment from an expensive one is built after the vessel berths — in terminal access charges, booking fees, empty container returns and free time that expires before a truck can physically collect the box.

Published 10 November 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

The Port You Choose Sets the Landed Cost

Importers tend to compare quotations on the one line item that looks comparable across all options: the ocean freight. On the Australia lane that comparison is misleading, because the ocean rate is the most uniform part of the quote and the landside is the most variable. Two carriers quoting within a few percent of each other can deliver to the same warehouse at materially different total cost, depending on which terminal the container lands at, how far the nominated empty container park sits from the delivery point, and whether the free-time clock starts at discharge or at collection.

Port selection is therefore a cost decision, not a routing preference: where the cargo is going, what the destination terminal and depot charge, and how much free time the carrier's tariff allows.

Melbourne is Australia's largest container port and handles more than a third of national container trade. Sydney moves the most valuable import mix but has the least room to expand landside. Brisbane is the growth gateway for Queensland, Fremantle a single-state port with long inland distances, and Adelaide small enough that cargo is frequently transhipped through Melbourne — adding a handling layer and a second set of charges.

What Happens After the Vessel Berths

The sequence is short and unforgiving. The container is discharged and becomes terminal-available; biosecurity and customs clearances are finalised; duty and GST are paid; a trucking company books a slot in the terminal's vehicle booking system; a truck collects; the empty is returned to a depot nominated by the carrier. Every one of those steps is billable, and several of them run on a clock that started at discharge rather than at collection.

This is where importers lose money. A container discharged on a Friday evening but blocked by a biosecurity inspection can consume a large share of its free time before any truck is entitled to take it, and the demurrage line simply arrives.

Terminal Access Charges and Booking Fees

Every container that passes a terminal gate in Australia now attracts charges that had no equivalent a decade ago. The terminal access charge is levied by the stevedore on the trucking company per container handled, and passes through to the importer in the cartage rate. On top sits a vehicle booking system fee per slot, with separate charges for late arrival, off-slot presentation and no-shows. Most terminals also apply a separate infrastructure surcharge that is reviewed annually.

The scale is not trivial. Across the five capital city ports, published terminal access charges for 2026 sit in the low hundreds of dollars per container, and combined landside charges — access, booking and empty return at a higher-cost depot — can reach several hundred dollars per container cycle before any cartage distance is considered. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission monitors stevedore charges annually precisely because they are set unilaterally by the terminal, not negotiated with the party that pays them.

Two consequences follow. Cartage quotes are only comparable if they show terminal access and booking fees explicitly, and the number of truck movements matters: a container collected first time and returned to a nearby depot costs far less than one turned away for a paperwork gap and rebooked.

Empty Container Parks: The Return Leg Nobody Prices

The empty return is the most consistently underestimated line in an Australian import. The carrier nominates an empty container park, and the trucking company pays a gate fee for each leg — one to collect, one to return. Those fees have risen sharply since they were introduced, and the spread between depots is wide: the same container can attract a gate fee two or three times higher at an expensive inner-city depot than at a cheaper one further out.

Three things are worth checking before the vessel sails. Which depot the carrier nominates, and whether an alternative is permitted. How far that depot is from the unpacking warehouse, because the return leg is a full truck movement in the wrong direction. And what time windows the depot operates, because peak-hour queuing at some parks routinely consumes an hour of truck time that is charged to somebody.

Where volumes justify it, negotiating an alternative depot into the rate agreement is one of the cheapest savings available on this lane.

Free Time, Demurrage and Detention

Australia runs three separate clocks, and confusing them is the single most common cause of avoidable cost.

ChargeBilled byTypical free period
Terminal storageThe stevedoreShort — often around three days from availability
DemurrageThe shipping lineRoughly three to seven days from discharge, depending on carrier
DetentionThe shipping lineRoughly seven to ten days from collection or vessel departure

Terminal storage is not demurrage. It is a separate invoice from a separate party for the same stationary box, and the two can overlap. Once free time expires, most Australian tariffs escalate in tiers rather than charging a flat daily rate, and 40-foot containers typically bill at a multiple of the 20-foot rate. Demurrage and detention are also subject to GST.

The counting rules diverge between carriers more than importers expect. Some count calendar days, so a weekend burns free time; some exclude the day of discharge, others count it; some publish one combined allowance, others keep two independent clocks. Only the carrier's published Australian tariff or your negotiated rate agreement answers the question, and it is worth reading before booking rather than after the invoice arrives.

Rail, Road and the Sydney Constraint

Port Botany is the clearest example of a port whose bottleneck is landside rather than wharfside. It handles a fraction of its installed capacity, and truck throughput is governed by a regulated appointment system: slots must be booked, arrival windows enforced, and both stevedores and carriers penalised for late arrival, no-shows and slow turnaround. Performance is monitored centrally and published, so landside discipline is measured rather than assumed.

Rail is absorbing a growing share of that pressure, with a record volume moving by train from the port's terminals in the last financial year. For importers with a western Sydney distribution centre, on-dock rail can remove a truck leg entirely. For everyone else, the practical response is to book slots as soon as the container is available, avoid Friday discharges where possible, and treat the trucking company's booking behaviour as a service level rather than a detail.

Documentation Discipline

Almost every avoidable storage charge traces back to a document, not to a vessel. A packing declaration that does not match the timber actually used, an ISPM 15 stamp that cannot be read, a goods description too vague for classification, a valuation that needs amendment — each produces a hold, and a hold produces a demurrage invoice.

The fix is sequencing. Lodge the full import declaration and the biosecurity documentation before the vessel arrives, confirm the empty return deadline on the day the container is collected, and photograph the container and its contents at the point of unpacking in case a claim is needed later. Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Comparing ocean rates only. Landside charges vary more between gateways than freight does.
  • Assuming terminal storage and demurrage are the same bill. They are separate invoices from separate parties.
  • Ignoring where the empty goes back. The return leg is a charged truck movement, often in the wrong direction.
  • Reading free time from general guidance. Only the carrier's tariff or your rate agreement governs your shipment.
  • Letting a document create the hold. Packing declarations and classifications are cheap to fix early and expensive to fix at the wharf.
