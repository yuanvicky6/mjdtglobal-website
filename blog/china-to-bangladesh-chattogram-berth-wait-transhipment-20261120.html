China to Bangladesh Sea Freight 2026: Chattogram Berth Waits, Transhipment & the Transit Buffer

Most China–Bangladesh ocean quotes stop at a port-to-port transit range. The variability sits at the destination: a tidal river port crossed only on a high tide, a feeder network that changes ship en route, and a berth queue that grows when the yard fills. This guide explains where the days go, what the 2026 Patenga expansion changes, and how to build a buffer a launch date can survive.

Published 20 November 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Why the Gateway, Not the Ocean Leg, Sets the Clock

A box from Shanghai, Ningbo, Shenzhen, Guangzhou or Qingdao spends most of its journey at sea, but that rarely decides the arrival date. The Bay of Bengal leg is schedulable; what is uncertain is the Chattogram call — tidal entry, whether the feeder connection was made, the anchorage queue, and yard space to discharge. A port-to-port figure describes the carrier's sailing plan, not the importer's door date. Quoted ranges of roughly two to four weeks silently assume a made connection and a free berth. Treat the ocean leg as a fixed cost of distance and the gateway as a separate risk budget.

A Tidal River Port, Not a Deep-Water Hub

Chattogram sits on the Karnaphuli River ten to twelve nautical miles inland from the Bay of Bengal and is a riverine port with a restricted draft. The outer bar's usable depth is not a fixed number: it follows the tide, the current permissible-draft notice and the vessel-specific pilot order. Agents work to a daylight ceiling of about nine to nine and a half metres, tightening at night and for longer ships, with less water at the older jetties. Semi-diurnal tides range from roughly two metres on a neap tide to over five on a spring tide, so a deep-draft vessel may have only hours around high water to cross.

Movements are timed to the tide, not to arrival. Inbound ships are routed from the Kutubdia anchorage to arrive on a rising tide, the harbour authority requires advance notice, and pilotage runs on daylight high-water windows rather than around the clock. A missed window means a wait of half a day or more. A ship too deep for the day's window lightens part of its cargo onto lighters at the outer anchorage, adding an operation and a dependency on lighter supply. That is why only modest ships, commonly cited around 2,800 TEU, berth directly; larger mainline vessels do not call.

Why Most Boxes Change Ship En Route

That draft ceiling makes this a transhipment lane. Most containerised cargo travels from China to a regional hub on a mainline service, then onward to Chattogram on a feeder; Colombo, Singapore and Port Klang are the hubs named consistently across carrier and forwarder descriptions. Direct calls are the exception, limited to vessels the river accepts.

Each transhipment is a handover: discharge at the hub, yard dwell, re-load to the feeder, and a connection the carrier must protect. If the mainline ship misses the feeder cut-off, the cargo rolls to the next sailing — days, not hours. Hub space is shared too; 2026 reporting described carriers allocating more capacity to Chinese exports and trimming bookings out of Chattogram, lengthening lead times. A shorter routing with two connections can arrive later than a longer one with a single reliable connection. Count the handovers, not just the headline transit.

Where the Days Actually Go

Congestion at Chattogram is a stack of ordinary delays rather than one shutdown:

  • Outer-anchorage queue. Vessels wait for a tidal window, a pilot slot or a berth; January 2026 reporting described over a hundred cargo ships waiting at the anchorage and the Kutubdia channel when lighter supply failed.
  • Yard density. Officials blame slow box collection; agents note that a gateway carrying most of the country's trade cannot run near yard capacity indefinitely. Volume grows against a fixed river port.
  • Release time. A national Time Release Study has historically measured cargo release in days, not hours, and the port has ranked near the bottom of international port performance indices. Discharge and release are separate clocks.
  • Labour and gate disruption. A late-January 2026 dispute over leasing a new terminal slowed daytime gate and transfer operations, adding one to two days to berthing expectations.
  • Weather. Heavy rain halts jetty work; cyclone risk can suspend anchorage operations with little notice.

Congestion trackers flagged the port again in late October 2026 with berthing delays in the tens of hours. These are signals, not a forecast: watch anchorage queue depth, lighter availability, yard occupancy and the working calendar.

Seasonality Worth Building Into the Plan

Two seasonal effects are predictable. From roughly June to September the south-west monsoon increases tidal amplitude, brings rain that interrupts discharge and raises cyclone risk; fog has also been cited in lighter shortages. Vessel numbers build ahead of Ramadan and Eid, and extended holidays compress customs working days, so boxes accumulate faster than they clear.

Widen the buffer inside those windows rather than avoiding them. Where a date is fixed, split the shipment: move launch-critical or line-stopping stock by air and let the balance follow by sea. What Patenga Changes, and What It Does Not

Capacity is being added in stages. The Patenga Container Terminal, at the Karnaphuli mouth, was built to berth ships closer to the sea and cut the upriver transit. Completed in 2022, it sat largely idle until an operator was appointed; under a 22-year concession it moved into full-scale operation around mid-2026, with reporting describing annual capacity rising from roughly a quarter of a million TEU toward six hundred thousand and new ship-to-shore cranes.

That improves berth availability but does not remove the outer bar, the tidal window at the older jetties, or the ceiling on vessel size. Further projects — the Bay Terminal, Laldia Char and the deeper Matarbari channel — are staged over years, and design figures are not current open-port availability. Plan against the constraints on the sailing date and confirm berth and draft conditions with the agent before fixing a vessel.

Mongla as a Partial Alternative

Mongla, the second seaport, is sometimes proposed as a relief valve. It suits cargo for the south-west and certain bulk or project moves, and some China-origin services quote it directly. But it is also riverine, reached up the Pussur Channel with its own draft limit, and its container handling and feeder connectivity are far smaller than Chattogram's. A diversion can trade a berth queue for a longer, thinner inland leg and less frequent sailings. Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Quoting the port-to-port range as a door date. It excludes tidal entry, the berth queue and the inland leg.
  • Choosing the shortest transit without counting handovers. Two transhipments can erase the saving.
  • Ignoring arrival draft. A ship too deep for the tide waits or lightens at the anchorage.
  • Assuming new berth capacity removes the river constraint. The outer bar and tidal window remain.
  • Clearing documents after the box lands. Yard dwell and release then compound the berth delay.
  • Planning across the monsoon or Eid with no slack. Both are predictable, not exceptional.
