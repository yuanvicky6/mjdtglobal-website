China to Bangladesh Project Cargo 2026: Heavy Machinery, OOG and Capital Equipment Shipping

A knitting machine, an injection moulding line, a diesel generator set, a transformer, a steel structure for a new shed: none of these behave like cargo that fits inside a standard box. They are lifted at points the manufacturer decides, they load the ground under the crane, they need road permits to leave the port, and they arrive with a start-up date attached. This guide sets out how project cargo from China to Bangladesh is actually engineered in 2026, where the port is the binding constraint, and which decisions belong to you instead of your forwarder.

Published 8 November 2026 · Reading time ~9 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Project Cargo Is a Different Product, Not a Bigger Container

Standard freight is priced per container or per cubic metre, and the unit is interchangeable. Project cargo is priced per piece, and no piece is interchangeable. The consequences cascade: the quotation depends on dimensions and centre of gravity, the vessel on lifting gear, the port on ground bearing capacity, and the delivery on a road survey.

That is why a machinery enquiry answered with a container rate is not an answer. Start from a technical data sheet per piece: dimensions, gross weight, lifting points, centre of gravity, whether it can be dismantled or must stay upright, whether it is weather-sensitive, and whether the manufacturer ships it crated or on a skid.

What Makes a Load Heavy or Out of Gauge

Out of gauge means outside the internal envelope of a container, and a load can be OOG on any single dimension. Width causes the most trouble, because it decides whether the piece can travel on an ordinary flatbed on an ordinary road. Height decides whether it clears bridges and overhead lines; length decides steering geometry on tight turns; weight decides crane class, axle loading and whether the factory approach road survives the journey.

The Binding Constraint Is the Port, Not the Ocean

Chattogram is a river port with tidal restrictions and modest channel depth, so vessel size and draft are already limited before cargo is discussed. For project cargo the sharper limit is the quay itself. Heavy breakbulk has historically been handled at the New Mooring Container Terminal jetty, a facility that was not designed for the purpose and is reported to bear around three tonnes per square metre. A piece that the ship's gear can lift may still be a piece the apron cannot support.

That constraint is being addressed. The Chittagong Port Authority has begun Bangladesh's first dedicated heavy-lift jetty at Laldia Char, a 240-metre facility with a draught of more than ten metres and a design load-bearing capacity of about five tonnes per square metre, built by the Bangladesh Navy under a project budget reported in the region of Tk 3 billion and scheduled for completion around the end of 2026. Until it is commissioned and proven in service, plan on the existing terminals and treat the new jetty as upside rather than assumption.

Container terminals are improving in parallel. Patenga Container Terminal, operated under a long-term concession, took delivery of ship-to-shore cranes rated for single-container lifts of around 40 tonnes and specialised lifts up to roughly 60 tonnes, with the operator targeting full-capacity operations from mid-2026. Useful — but a 60-tonne crane rating is not a 60-tonne cargo permission: the lift still has to satisfy the ground, the outreach, the lashing plan and the port's approval.

Choosing the Vessel and the Lift Plan

Three families of option exist, and weight drives the choice. Container service with flat racks and open tops is cheapest and most frequent, and suits machinery that breaks down into shippable modules. Breakbulk vessels take indivisible pieces with ship's gear, but calls at Chattogram are infrequent, so the sailing window becomes a constraint to design around. Heavy-lift vessels need a berth that can accept them and a lift plan approved in advance.

Whichever is chosen, the lift plan prevents damage and delay: it names the lifting points, sling angle, spreader or beam, crane class at each end, and who signs off. Where the manufacturer's lugs are unsuitable — common on machines designed for factory installation rather than sea transport — that conversation belongs in China, not on the quay.

The Inland Leg for Indivisible Loads

The Chattogram-to-factory move is where most project schedules slip, because a load that was awkward at sea becomes a permit problem on land. Over-dimensional movements typically require road authority permission, escorts and travel-hour restrictions; bridges and culverts have weight and height limits that are not published consistently; and the final approach to a factory gate is often narrower than the highway and flanked by walls, trees or cabling that nobody surveyed.

The remedy is a route survey before the vessel sails, not after discharge. Someone should physically check the corridor from the terminal to the factory, record overhead clearances, weighbridge availability, turning radii, and where the load can be parked if a permit slips a day. On a factory expansion, the site itself needs checking too: crane standing area, ground bearing where the machine will be set down, and whether the shed roof is closed before the largest piece arrives.

Duty, Concessions and Why Classification Comes First

Capital machinery is treated favourably in Bangladesh, but the favourable treatment is conditional and conditional treatment is where money is lost. Concessions for capital machinery, spare parts and raw materials for export industries run through SROs, and each carries conditions — bond status, end use, registration with the investment authority, sometimes a certificate that the item is a capital asset rather than a consumable.

The order of work follows from this. Classify and confirm the concession before the purchase order, because the landed cost decides whether the project is viable. Then build the document pack: invoice with precise descriptions, packing list by package number, bill of lading matching those numbers, certificate of origin, insurance certificate, import registration certificate and BIN, and the test reports your bank and engineer require.

Packing, Insurance and the Origin Inspection

Machinery is damaged less by the sea than by movement, moisture and handling. Vapour barriers and desiccant, internal bracing that stops a heavy module shifting inside its crate, and shock or tilt indicators on anything delicate are ordinary measures, frequently skipped when a supplier packs in a hurry. Photograph the packing in progress and at handover; it is the only evidence that settles a claim later.

Insurance should be arranged against the actual cargo value and route, including the inland leg, intermediate storage and time on the quay. A policy written generically for containerised goods may not respond to a lifting accident on breakbulk discharge, so declare the cargo nature and lift arrangements up front.

At origin, a pre-shipment inspection and, where the contract allows, a factory acceptance test are cheaper than any second attempt. Verifying that the machine runs, that voltage and frequency match Bangladeshi supply and that the spare-parts list is complete takes days in China and months to fix in Bangladesh.

The Sequence That Protects a Start-Up Date

  1. Technical data first. Dimensions, weight, lifting points and centre of gravity per package, in writing, before a rate is requested.
  2. Route and site survey. Terminal to gate, gate to factory, factory to installation position.
  3. Classify and cost the duty. Confirm SRO eligibility and the combined incidence before committing to the order.
  4. Choose the mode against the piece, not the calendar. Broken-down modules on container service versus indivisible pieces on breakbulk.
  5. Approve the lift plan and the lashing plan. With named equipment and named responsibility at both ends.
  6. Pre-book inland permits and the crane. A mobile crane booked for the week after arrival is a crane booked after demurrage has started.
  7. Commission in the right order. Utilities, foundations and roof closure before the largest item enters the building.

Mistakes That Recur on Machinery Shipments

  • Quoting a machine as if it were a pallet. Volume-based pricing collapses the moment width becomes the constraint.
  • Trusting the manufacturer's lifting lugs. They may be designed for a factory crane, not a port lift.
  • Assuming the new heavy-lift terminal is already working. Plan on current facilities and treat new capacity as upside.
  • Skipping the route survey. A permit issue found at the factory gate costs more than the survey.
  • Packing for a warehouse, not for a voyage. Moisture and internal movement account for most avoidable damage.
  • Discovering the concession conditions at assessment. SRO conditions are checked when the entry is filed, not when it is appealed.
  • Letting the ship date become the project date. Installation is the milestone; the vessel is only one input to it.
