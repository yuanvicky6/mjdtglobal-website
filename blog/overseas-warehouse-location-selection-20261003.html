Overseas Warehouse Location Strategy 2026: Choosing US, EU, UK & Australia Fulfillment Nodes

Most sellers choose an overseas warehouse the way they choose a courier: whoever the platform recommends, wherever the biggest 3PL advertises. Six months later they discover the real problem — the warehouse was in the wrong country, the wrong coast, or the wrong tax jurisdiction for where their orders actually come from. Node selection is the highest-leverage decision in overseas-warehouse strategy, because it fixes your delivery speed, your freight-in routing and your compliance footprint for years. This guide sets out a 2026 methodology: map demand first, then pick nodes — with the trade-offs for the US, the EU, the UK and Australia spelled out.

Published 3 October 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Start With the Demand Map, Not the Warehouse

Before comparing warehouses, export twelve months of orders and plot them. For each market, ask three questions: where do buyers cluster geographically, what do they buy (which SKUs sell in which country), and how delivery-sensitive are they — does a marketplace badge for fast shipping actually move conversion, or is the category patient?

The demand map usually kills one of two fantasies. It kills the "one warehouse for the world" fantasy when 60% of orders turn out to sit on the US East Coast while the warehouse is in Los Angeles. And it kills the "warehouse in every market" fantasy when a second-country node would carry 8% of volume at double the fixed overhead. Selection is an empirical exercise, and the data already exists in your order export.

Single Node vs Multi-Node: The Real Trade-Off

Every additional node buys delivery speed and customs simplicity in one market, and charges you in duplicated inventory, split replenishment and extra management overhead. The honest comparison:

FactorSingle nodeMulti-node
Inventory capitalOne pool, one safety stockEvery node needs its own safety stock — capital multiplies
ReplenishmentOne inbound lane from ChinaEach node is a separate forecast; misallocation means stockouts in one node, overstock in another
Delivery promiseFast near the node, slower at the edgesFast everywhere you have a node
Customs & taxOne import regimeOne import regime per country — each with its own registration and filings
Operational loadOne WMS integration, one SLAMultiplied integrations, SLAs and failure points

The pattern that works for most mid-size sellers: one node per major market, added one at a time, each justified by enough order density to carry its own safety stock. Add the second node when the first one is measurably losing orders at its geographic edges — not when a 3PL's sales deck says so.

The US: East, West or Central

The United States is large enough that node placement inside the country matters as much as the decision to warehouse there at all. West Coast nodes (Los Angeles / Ontario corridor) sit closest to the trans-Pacific inbound lane, minimising freight-in cost and time, but deliver slowly to the Northeast population belt. East Coast nodes (New Jersey / Pennsylvania corridor) sit on the demand but take longer ocean transit or require Panama routing. Central nodes (Texas, the Midwest) split the difference on domestic delivery coverage.

The inbound-vs-outbound tension is the whole game: West Coast optimises the China leg, East Coast optimises the last mile. For sellers whose demand skews East Coast — common for general merchandise — an East or dual-coast structure often wins despite the longer inbound, because domestic parcel cost compounds on every order while the ocean leg is paid once per restock. Domestic parcel zones and surcharges should be modelled against your actual order map before committing; a forwarder or 3PL can run this comparison from your order export.

Europe: One Hub or Country Nodes

The EU can theoretically be served from a single hub — Germany or the Netherlands are the classic choices, sitting at the centre of European road networks with strong ocean and rail connectivity. For most sellers, one well-placed EU hub is the right answer for years: goods cleared into free circulation in one member state move within the EU without repeated customs events.

The complications are VAT and demand spread. Import VAT treatment differs by member state and by scheme, distance-selling and one-stop-shop rules determine where VAT is accounted for on B2C sales, and marketplace deemed-supplier rules may already be handling part of the compliance for you. None of this forces a country node by itself — but it does mean the hub decision should be made together with your VAT registration strategy, not separately. If your EU volume is concentrated in one large market (often Germany, sometimes France or Italy), a hub inside that market simplifies both delivery and VAT. The UK: Always a Separate Node

Post-Brexit, the UK is a third country relative to the EU. Goods in an EU warehouse are exports when they cross to a UK buyer, with customs declarations and potential duty on both sides; UK demand cannot be served "for free" from an EU hub. If UK volume is material, a UK node — typically in the Midlands corridor — is a separate decision with its own import VAT scheme and its own restock lane from China. The frequent mistake is discovering this after launch, when a fifth of orders suddenly need customs paperwork the EU hub cannot provide.

Australia and Secondary Markets

Australia concentrates its population in a handful of metro areas, so one East Coast node — Sydney or Melbourne — covers most of the parcel market with acceptable transit to Brisbane, Adelaide and Perth. The bigger constraint is biosecurity at import: wooden packaging and contaminated containers draw treatment orders, so the Australia inbound lane needs stricter origin preparation than most. Our Australia LCL and 3PL guide covers the local fulfilment side.

Secondary markets — the Gulf, South Africa, Brazil, South East Asia — deserve nodes only when volume is both large and durable. Below that threshold, ship cross-border from China on a DDP express or consolidation lane and revisit annually.

Tax and Compliance Footprints

Every node is a tax footprint. Import registrations, VAT or GST numbers, and in the US, potential sales-tax nexus triggered by inventory held in a state, all attach to the physical stock. Three disciplines keep this manageable: register before the first inbound, not after the first assessment; keep node decisions and tax registrations in the same conversation; and review nexus positions whenever you add or move stock. Marketplaces increasingly withhold or remit on your behalf in some regimes, but that does not remove your import-side obligations — confirm the current position with your accountant for each market you enter.

The Hidden Cost of Every Extra Node: Inventory Spread

The cost that kills multi-node strategies is rarely rent or parcel rates — it is capital trapped in the wrong place. Two nodes each carrying safety stock for the same SKU hold more total inventory than one node serving the same demand, because demand variability does not average out across split pools. Every restock becomes an allocation decision, and allocation mistakes surface as stockouts in one node and aged stock in another. Before adding a node, check that you can forecast per node, not just per market — if the forecasting discipline is not there yet, the extra node will convert forecasting weakness into physical overstock.

A Seven-Question Selection Checklist

  1. Where are my buyers, by weight of orders rather than by country label?
  2. Which SKUs sell in which market — does the assortment even justify local stock there?
  3. What delivery promise does my channel actually reward in that market?
  4. Does one node cover the demand cluster, or does the geography genuinely split?
  5. Can I fund and forecast a separate safety stock per node?
  6. What import, VAT/GST or sales-tax registrations does this node trigger?
  7. Is there a restock lane from China that fits my replenishment cadence (sea, rail, air)?

Common Pitfalls

  • Choosing the warehouse before mapping demand. The order export answers the question; sales decks do not.
  • Adding a second node before the first is tight. Every node needs its own safety stock and forecast.
  • Serving the UK from an EU hub. It works until customs reality arrives with the first order.
  • Ignoring the inbound lane. A node is only as good as its restock cadence from China.
  • Treating tax registration as a later problem. Register before the first inbound container, not after the first assessment notice.
