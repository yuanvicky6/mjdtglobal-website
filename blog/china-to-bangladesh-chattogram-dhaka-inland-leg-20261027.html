China to Bangladesh Inland Leg 2026: Chattogram to Dhaka by Road, Rail or River Barge

Ocean freight from China to Chattogram is, by comparison, the manageable part. The fragile part is what happens next: a container discharged at a port that handles nearly all of the country's seaborne trade, then moved a few hundred kilometres to Dhaka or the industrial belt under short free time, a congested highway, and documents that must be perfect before a truck moves. This guide explains how the inland leg is structured in 2026, how the three modes differ, and which decisions reduce cost rather than simply shifting it.

Published 27 October 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Why the Inland Leg Decides the Landed Cost

Chattogram handles the overwhelming majority of Bangladesh's seaborne cargo, and roughly four fifths of the containers passing through it are destined for Dhaka and its surrounding industrial districts. Almost all of that volume leaves by road. Rail accounts for a small single-digit share, and the river route through Pangaon has historically carried around one percent — a share so small that the underused terminal became a standing example of infrastructure without traffic.

The result is a concentration of risk in one corridor. When the Dhaka–Chattogram highway slows, so does every factory input and every export consignment. When the port's yards fill, free time disappears and storage charges start. When a strike or a monsoon closure interrupts lighterage at the outer anchorage, vessels queue and demurrage accrues on ships that cannot work. None of this appears in an ocean freight quote, and all of it appears in the invoice you receive after arrival.

Road: The Default, and Its Failure Modes

Road is the default because it goes everywhere and needs no schedule. The cost is that it inherits every problem on the corridor: the highway's capacity, the behaviour of a few thousand trucks at the port gates, and the calendar. During public holidays — Eid in particular — driver availability collapses, gates slow, and transit estimates that looked reasonable in a quotation become fiction. Political and labour stoppages can stop container delivery almost entirely for days, after which the backlog takes longer to clear than the stoppage itself.

For an importer, the practical control is sequencing rather than speed. Book the truck before the box is released, confirm the factory can receive it at the promised time, and treat any delivery promise that ignores the holiday calendar as an internal target rather than a customer commitment.

Rail: Kamalapur ICD and the Locomotive Constraint

Rail freight between Chattogram and Dhaka has been constrained for a structural reason: not enough metre-gauge locomotives to run container trains at the frequency the port and the ICD were designed around. Reporting through 2026 shows container trains arriving at Kamalapur well below the volumes the port authority has asked for, with importers and shipping lines paying for the gap through longer ICD dwell and, in some cases, rerouting.

Rail remains useful when it works — it bypasses the highway entirely and consolidates many boxes into one movement. But it should be planned as a scheduled product with confirmed slots, not as an assumption you build a delivery date on. If your consignee's production plan depends on rail, keep a road fallback costed and ready.

River: Pangaon and the Barge Revival

Pangaon Inland Container Terminal sits on the river inside the Dhaka metropolitan area, close to much of the garment and consumer-goods demand that the port of Chattogram serves. Inaugurated more than a decade ago, it spent years operating far below capacity. That changed in 2026: a new private operator took a long-term concession and reopened the terminal with scheduled barge services synchronised to mainline vessel calls, a bonded warehouse on site, reefer plugs, and its own first- and last-mile trucking.

For a shipper this is the most interesting development on the corridor in years, because the barge leg removes the highway from the equation and typically prices below road haulage — the gap is reported to be widest on export boxes, which is why the operator's early growth came largely from exporters. Two caveats keep it honest. Barge capacity is still small relative to the road corridor, and sailings are counted in a few per week rather than per day. And a river route adds a terminal handover at each end, so the saving on line-haul has to survive two sets of handling charges and a schedule that must line up with the vessel.

Off-Dock ICDs and CFS: Where Your Box Actually Waits

A large share of Chattogram's container handling happens outside the port gates, at private off-dock inland container depots and container freight stations. Exports are stuffed and staged there before moving to the terminal; imports are frequently devanned or held there after discharge. For an LCL importer this is where the cargo physically becomes yours, and it is also where charges accumulate quietly: CFS handling, storage beyond free days, and the labour cost of waiting for a document that should have been issued in China.

Ask for the off-dock tariff in writing before booking, and ask which depot. A quote that names a port but not a depot is not a quote you can compare.

Two implications follow. Classification and valuation questions should be settled before the vessel sails, because they are cheapest to fix at origin. And statutory clearance windows exist, with uncleared consignments eventually dealt with by the state rather than by you — the real reason free time is a commercial issue rather than an administrative one.

Free Time, Demurrage and Detention

Three different clocks run at once: free time at the terminal or depot before storage begins, demurrage on the container while it sits full, and detention once it has left the port empty and late. Published free time at Chattogram is short, and effective free time is shorter still when yards are congested or customs is backlogged. A single wet week in the monsoon season can consume an entire allowance.

The control that works is a countdown, not a reminder: date of discharge, date the bill of entry must be filed, date of release, date the truck must be at the gate. Programmes that manage the inland leg well run that countdown from the moment the vessel is confirmed, and they know in advance which consignments can be devanned at an off-dock instead of waiting for a factory slot.

Seasonality Worth Planning Around

Bangladesh's calendar has three recurring pressure points. The monsoon, roughly June to September, brings flooding on the corridor and cyclone risk in the Bay of Bengal; lighterage at the outer anchorage can be suspended for days, and low-lying depots have flooded with cargo inside. The Eid holidays compress demand and remove labour at the same time. And the pre-holiday import rush fills yards precisely when everyone needs them empty. Build buffer into transit commitments for those windows rather than discovering the pattern in a service failure.

Five Decisions to Take Before Booking

  1. Mode by consignee, not by habit. River suits Dhaka-city clusters; road suits dispersed factories; rail suits neither unless a slot is confirmed.
  2. Name the depot. Port-level quotes hide the off-dock charges that decide the final number.
  3. Fix the document pack at origin. Consistency across invoice, B/L, packing list and origin certificate is cheaper than any corrective fee.
  4. Agree who owns the countdown. Someone must track discharge, filing, release and gate-in as one chain.
  5. Decide the fallback. For every rail or barge plan, cost the road alternative in advance and set the trigger date for switching.

Mistakes That Recur

  • Quoting ocean freight as a delivered cost. The inland leg, port charges and depot handling are separate lines for a reason.
  • Assuming rail capacity. Train availability is a constraint, not a schedule you can rely on.
  • Filing the declaration after discharge. Free time is counted from discharge, not from your convenience.
  • Ignoring the holiday calendar. A booking made for the week of Eid is a booking made for the week after.
  • Letting the supplier describe the goods loosely. Vague descriptions create classification and examination problems downstream.
  • Underestimating monsoon buffer. One flooded week costs more than a season of slightly conservative promises.
