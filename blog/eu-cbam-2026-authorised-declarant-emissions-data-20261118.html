EU CBAM 2026: Authorised Declarant, the 50-Tonne Threshold & Emissions Data for China Exporters

The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism stopped being a quarterly reporting exercise on 1 January 2026 and became a customs condition. Cargo in scope now needs an authorised declarant behind it, and the first certificate surrender for the 2026 import year falls due on 30 September 2027. This guide explains who carries the obligation, when the 50-tonne threshold exempts an importer, how embedded emissions are evidenced and verified, and what a DDP arrangement into Europe changes about all of it.

Published 18 November 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

What Actually Changed in 2026

CBAM has been law since 2023 under Regulation (EU) 2023/956, but the transitional phase from October 2023 to December 2025 required only quarterly emissions reporting, with no money attached. From 1 January 2026 the mechanism mirrors the EU Emissions Trading System financially: emissions embedded in covered imports carry a carbon price.

Regulation (EU) 2025/2083, adopted in October 2025, simplified the definitive regime before it started — most importantly by introducing the mass-based threshold and by pushing certificate sales to February 2027. The practical effect is that 2026 is a build year: imports must be registered correctly and data assembled, but no certificates change hands this calendar year.

Who Carries the Obligation

The declarant is the EU importer of record. Where an overseas seller or its agent files as the importer of record — the structure many DDP arrangements create — that entity, or the indirect customs representative acting for it, becomes the authorised CBAM declarant and must hold a CBAM account number or application reference number.

  • Authorisation is a precondition for release. Above the threshold, clearances without valid declarant status are exposed to refusal and to the higher penalty band.
  • The threshold belongs to the importer, not the shipment. It is measured per importer across a calendar year, and the supplier's own export volume is irrelevant to it.
  • Hydrogen and electricity never benefit from the exemption — an authorised declarant is always required.

The 50-Tonne Threshold, Stated Precisely

An importer bringing in CBAM goods other than hydrogen and electricity is exempt from authorisation while annual imports stay below 50 tonnes by mass. Cross that line and the full obligation applies to the year's imports. Two consequences catch sellers out: low-volume programme launches can sit under the threshold for a season and then cross it mid-year, and a single heavy SKU — a machine frame, a coil, a structural component — can cross it in one shipment.

Mass, not value and not emissions, is the measure. A dense, low-value consignment of steel fittings triggers the regime far sooner than a high-value aluminium accessory set of the same tonne count would.

Scope Today and the 2028 Expansion

Six sectors are covered by CN code: cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen. Indirect emissions are in scope for cement and fertilisers under the definitive methodology; other sectors continue on direct emissions only at this stage.

The Commission has proposed extending the regime to roughly 180 downstream steel- and aluminium-intensive product codes from 2028. That is the change that will pull ordinary consumer and industrial exporters into scope, because it targets the metal content of finished goods rather than the metal itself.

The Calendar That Matters: 2027

The definitive regime runs on an annual cycle, with one year's lag between import and settlement.

  • 1 February 2027: certificate sales open. Certificates covering 2026 imports can be bought from this date.
  • Quarterly from 2027: declarants must hold certificates covering at least half of the embedded emissions imported since the start of the year, bought each quarter.
  • 30 September 2027: the annual CBAM declaration for 2026 imports is filed in the CBAM registry, and the matching number of certificates surrendered on the same date.
  • After surrender: surplus certificates can be bought back by the competent authority, which removes some of the risk from over-buying.

The takeaway for a Chinese supplier is that the data you provide during 2027 is being used to settle a 2026 liability. Documentation that is not assembled while the production batch is fresh is expensive to reconstruct.

How the Charge Is Calculated

The price of a certificate tracks the EU ETS: a quarterly average in 2026, moving to a weekly average from 2027. The first reference price published in April 2026 was EUR 75.36 per tonne of CO2e, which gives a sense of the order of magnitude without committing anyone to a figure.

Two adjustments sit between embedded emissions and the bill. The free-allocation benchmark still shields part of the emissions, scaled by a CBAM factor that starts at 0.975 for 2026 and declines each year toward 2034, when embedded emissions are covered in full. Any carbon price already paid in the country of production can be deducted where it is evidenced. Because the factor and the benchmarks both move annually, the same product's liability is not stable year to year.

Default Values or Actual Values

Declarants can fall back on Commission-published default values per product group, or use verified actual values from the producing installation. Default values need no verification and no supplier cooperation, which is why they are common in the first year — and why they are frequently the more expensive choice.

Defaults are set at conservative levels, so a reasonably efficient plant can end up paying for emissions it did not produce. Actual values require a monitoring plan, an emissions report and a verifier accredited by a national accreditation body, with the verifier independent of anyone providing the calculation or filing service. Accreditation began in 2026 and the verifier registry opened in September 2026, so capacity is still building. The commercial decision is straightforward: compare the certificate saving from verified data against the cost of obtaining it, per product group, before assuming either answer.

Getting Data Out of a Chinese Supply Chain

This is where most programmes stall. CBAM calculates by functional unit — one tonne of goods under one eight-digit CN code — and complex goods inherit the embedded emissions of their precursors. A steel fabricator needs the mill's figures; an aluminium product assembler needs the smelter's and the extruder's.

  • Contract for data, not just goods. Specify the emissions data, format and delivery date in the purchase agreement, with a remedy if it is missing.
  • Map precursors before the first RFQ. Know which inputs carry reportable emissions and which suppliers hold the records.
  • Use the free official tooling. China's Single Window carries a CBAM filing assistant built on a database of covered products and precursor materials, which reduces the reporting fields dramatically for routine cases.
  • Keep the underlying records. Authorities can reconstruct declarations years later; the monitoring trail is the defence.

Penalties and Enforcement

Certificates not surrendered by the deadline attract a penalty of EUR 100 per tonne of CO2e, and the surrender obligation survives the penalty — you pay twice. Imports by a non-authorised declarant carry three to five times that rate, and serious or repeated breaches can cost an importer its declarant status, which in practice closes the border to CBAM goods.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Assuming consumer goods are out of scope forever. The 2028 downstream extension targets metal content in finished products.
  • Treating the threshold as per shipment. It is annual and per importer, and dense cargo crosses it quickly.
  • Running DDP without a declarant. If the seller is the importer of record, declarant status is the seller's obligation.
  • Defaulting to default values by reflex. They are convenient and often cost more than verified data.
  • Chasing verification too late. Accredited verifier capacity is still forming, and verification happens at the plant.
  • Reconstructing data a year later. Emissions records are cheapest while the production run is live.
