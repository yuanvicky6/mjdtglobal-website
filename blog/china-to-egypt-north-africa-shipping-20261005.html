China to Egypt & North Africa Shipping 2026: Alexandria, ACID Pre-Registration & Cairo Delivery

North Africa behaves least like the rest of the continent. Egypt, Morocco, Tunisia and Algeria sit on Mediterranean lanes with week-scale transit from China, run customs regimes modelled on European practice, and buy consumer goods in volumes that rival mid-size European markets. They also carry a requirement most first-time exporters discover too late: Egypt requires cargo information to be registered before the vessel loads, and a container that sails without it cannot be cleared. This guide covers the lane in 2026 — gateways, ACID pre-registration, conformity, duty and VAT, FX reality and last-mile delivery.

Published 5 October 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

North Africa Is Not One Market

Treating the region as a single lane is the first mistake. Egypt is the volume market — roughly a hundred million consumers, a large manufacturing base, and Mediterranean ports with direct services from China. Morocco has a more open trade posture, strong European integration and a growing automotive and textile sector. Tunisia is smaller, with a Euro-linked economy and tight import controls. Algeria is the most restrictive of the four, with import licensing and heavy FX controls that make it a specialist lane rather than a standard one.

Practical consequence: pick the country first, then the port, then the mode. A forwarder quoting "North Africa" as one rate is telling you they have not done the compliance work.

ACID: File Before the Vessel Loads

Egypt's advance cargo information requirement — known by its acronym ACID — is the single most important process on this lane. The importer registers the shipment in the Egyptian customs single-window system before the goods are loaded in China, and the resulting registration number must appear on the shipping documents. A consignment that arrives without a matching registration cannot be cleared and typically has to be returned or held, at the importer's cost.

The operational sequence is therefore inverted relative to most lanes: confirm the importer's registration details first, then book the shipment, then load. In practice this means:

  1. Importer registration in the single-window system — the Egyptian consignee must be set up and able to generate the ACID record.
  2. Shipment details exchanged before booking — exporter's and importer's identifiers, invoice data, HS codes, quantities, vessel and port details.
  3. Registration issued, then matched to documents — the number must appear consistently on the bill of lading and commercial documents.
  4. Load only after confirmation — no registration means no clearance, whatever the commercial urgency.

Because the Egyptian consignee owns most of this workflow, choose buyers carefully and confirm their registration capability before the first order. A reliable forwarder will chase the ACID number actively rather than discovering its absence at the port.

Conformity, Standards and Inspection

Egypt requires conformity certification for a wide range of imported consumer and industrial goods, administered through the Egyptian standards and export/import control authority (GOEIC). Depending on the category, that means a certificate from an approved inspection body, a test report against Egyptian standards, or a pre-shipment inspection at origin. Categories commonly affected include electrical and electronic goods, textiles and apparel, toys, food-contact items and building materials.

The logistics lesson is the same as on other regulated lanes: certification is an origin-side process. Establishing which standard applies — and booking the inspection — before production finishes costs days; discovering the requirement after arrival costs weeks and storage. Regulated categories and recognised certification routes change periodically, so confirm the current requirement for your specific HS line with your clearing agent before shipping.

Duty, VAT and Valuation

Egyptian imports are assessed on the customs value with a tariff duty that varies by HS line, plus value added tax at the standard rate applied to the dutiable base. Rates and category surcharges are revised by decree, so the practical rule is to classify accurately and let the local broker compute the current stack. What matters commercially is that tax is charged on customs value plus duty, so your declared value drives both layers. Under-declaration is the classic false economy: valuation checks are routine and the reassessment plus penalties lands on your buyer.

FX and Payment Terms: The Real Constraint

For several years the binding constraint on Egyptian import trade has not been freight capacity but foreign exchange. Egypt has periodically tightened access to hard currency, which affects how importers pay Chinese suppliers and how quickly. Documentary collections and letters of credit are common, and importers may face queues or prioritisation for FX allocation depending on the period and the goods category.

For an exporter this argues for terms that reflect reality: agree the instrument and timing explicitly (sight versus usance LC, or deposit plus balance), and price in the financing cost of longer cash cycles. Sellers who assume European payment terms find their working capital funding an FX queue they cannot see. Confirm the current FX climate with your buyer and their bank before quoting.

Choosing the Mode

Sea is the default for anything of volume: Mediterranean services from Chinese ports reach Alexandria in weeks, and FCL beats LCL on unit cost above modest volumes once ACID and conformity are done. Air into Cairo is reserved for samples, high-value electronics and restocks where a week of delay costs more than the freight premium. Consolidation is the middle path: batching several suppliers into one consignment amortises clearance and certification cost across SKUs, which matters on Egypt's regulated lines. Choose the mode after the ACID timeline is understood — the registration workflow adds days before loading.

Beyond Egypt: Morocco, Tunisia, Algeria

Morocco runs a comparatively open import regime through Casablanca and Tanger Med, with strong logistics infrastructure and heavy European integration — for European-distribution strategies it is often as much a European node as an African one. Tunisia is smaller, with tighter import administration and Euro-linked trade. Algeria is the specialist case: import-licence regimes and significant FX controls make it a lane for sellers with a committed local partner. Each of the four warrants its own compliance review rather than a shared regional playbook.

Last Mile: Cairo, Giza, Alexandria

Greater Cairo — including Giza and the new administrative districts east of the city — concentrates the country's parcel volume, followed by Alexandria and the Delta cities. Address quality is variable outside planned compounds, so a reachable phone number and a landmark-based delivery instruction function better than a street address alone. Cash on delivery remains common for B2C, which means couriers collect and remit, and remittance cycles belong in the cash-flow model. B2B deliveries to factories and warehouses in the industrial zones are straightforward when the clearance paperwork is complete; when it is not, cargo sits in port storage where charges accrue daily.

Common Pitfalls on the Egypt and North Africa Lane

  • Loading before the ACID registration exists. The single most expensive mistake on this lane — the cargo simply cannot clear.
  • Assuming conformity is a destination problem. Inspection and certification belong at origin, before loading.
  • Ignoring the FX dimension in payment terms. Terms that work with a European buyer can leave you financing a currency queue.
  • Quoting "North Africa" as one lane. Egypt, Morocco, Tunisia and Algeria have materially different regimes.
  • Under-declaring value. Valuation checks are routine and the penalty lands on your buyer.
  • Shipping B2C with an address-only delivery instruction. Phone contact and landmarks decide whether the parcel lands.
