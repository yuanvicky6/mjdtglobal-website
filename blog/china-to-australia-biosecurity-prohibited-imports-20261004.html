Australia Biosecurity & Prohibited Imports 2026: DAFF, BICON & Treatment Rules for China Cargo

Australia runs one of the strictest biosecurity regimes in the world, and it is the reason otherwise-clean shipments from China get stopped, treated and billed at the border. An island continent with a fragile agricultural economy protects itself at the container door: wooden packaging, straw filler, seed-containing goods, food items, used machinery and even container-floor contamination can each trigger a treatment order that costs more than the freight. This guide explains how the system works in 2026 — how screening happens, which categories are high-risk, what a treatment order costs you, and how to prepare cargo so it clears without drama.

Published 4 October 2026 · Reading time ~8 min · By MJDT Global Logistics Team

Why Australia Is the Strictest Lane You Ship

Most customs regimes care about what you owe. Australian biosecurity cares about what you might be carrying — insects, seeds, soil, plant and animal material, contaminants that could establish themselves in a country whose ecosystem and farm sector have no defences against them. The department responsible (the agriculture and biosecurity arm of government, referred to here as DAFF, its long-standing name) has the authority to direct containers for inspection, order treatment, or order re-export and destruction, with all costs on the importer.

For Chinese-origin cargo the practical consequence is that biosecurity is a pre-shipment discipline, not a customs-day event. Everything that determines whether your container gets flagged — packaging material, cleanliness, product composition, the honesty of your packing declaration — is decided in the Chinese factory and warehouse before loading.

How Screening Actually Works

Screening runs on documents first and physical inspection second. The biosecurity officer reviews the import declaration, the packing declaration and any treatment certificates attached to the consignment; only a subset of containers is physically inspected, chosen by risk profiling. That is why paperwork quality is the first line of defence: a complete, accurate packing declaration with valid treatment certificates attached steers the consignment through; a vague or missing one practically invites an inspection.

When inspection does happen it is usually at the container level — tail inspection at the terminal, or unpacking at an approved premises for higher-risk cargo. Inspectors look at the container itself (floor, walls, doors for contamination and insects), the packaging (pallets, crates, dunnage and their ISPM 15 stamps), and the goods. Finding bark, insects, straw, soil or seeds anywhere in that chain triggers a direction.

BICON: Check Before You Ship

BICON is the public biosecurity database that answers the only question that matters: "can I bring this in, and under what conditions?" Every importer shipping goods with any plant, animal, wood or food component should search BICON for the commodity before placing the purchase order — not before the shipment, before the order. Some goods are prohibited outright; some enter only with specific treatment or documentation; some enter freely. The conditions listed in BICON are exactly what the officer will enforce at the border, so the database is effectively your compliance specification.

For e-commerce sellers the habit is the same at parcel scale: a wooden craft, a seed-containing product or a food item is BICON-searchable regardless of whether it moves by container or by post. The rules do not soften because the consignment is small — if anything, small parcels with misleading declarations are flagged more aggressively.

Wooden Packaging and ISPM 15

Pallets, crates, cases and dunnage made of solid wood must be ISPM 15 compliant — heat-treated or fumigated by a certified facility and stamped with the IPPC mark. The stamp must be legible and on every wood component. Australian inspectors read pallet stamps the way customs officers read HS codes: a missing or unreadable mark is treated as non-compliant wood, and non-compliant wood is a treatment order.

Two cheap substitutes remove the risk entirely: engineered wood products (plywood, OSB, pressed board) are generally outside the solid-wood rules, and plastic pallets are outside them completely. For repeat lanes, standardising on compliant or non-wood packaging is cheaper than a single treatment event. Also check the container itself before loading — mud on the floor or plant debris from a previous cargo becomes the importer's problem at the Australian end.

Treatment Orders: What They Cost You

When biosecurity finds a problem, the options are treatment (fumigation or heat treatment by an approved provider), export out of Australia, or destruction — all at the importer's expense, plus storage while the direction is arranged. The direct charges are only part of the bill: treatment slots are not instant, and cargo typically waits at a depot accruing storage while the direction is processed and the treatment booked. A single direction routinely costs more than the entire ocean freight of the consignment.

Insurance rarely covers it, because the root cause is a compliance failure rather than a transit casualty. This is why origin-side discipline is not a cost — it is the cheapest insurance on the lane.

Packing Declarations and Container Cleanliness

The packing declaration is the document by which the packer states, among other things, what packaging materials were used and that the container was clean before loading. It must be accurate — a declaration that says "no straw" while straw filler sits between cartons is not just rejected, it damages the importer's profile for every future consignment. Keep declarations specific, dated, and matched to what was actually packed; attach ISPM 15 and treatment certificates to the same document set so the officer sees a coherent story rather than a puzzle.

E-Commerce Parcels: The Same Rules, Smaller Margin for Error

Express and postal parcels are screened at the mail and courier gateways, and the categories that stop containers stop parcels: seeds and plants (a very common listing mistake), wooden crafts and instruments, food and supplements, animal products, and anything with a misleading commercial-invoice description ("gift" with no detail is a flag, not a shield). Sellers shipping B2C into Australia should run every new SKU through BICON at listing time, describe goods accurately on invoices, and treat wooden or agricultural-component products as high-touch items requiring verified compliance before sale. What Happens When a Shipment Fails

  1. A direction is issued to the importer of record: treat, export or destroy.
  2. The cargo moves to an approved arrangement premises — more handling, more cost, more time.
  3. Treatment or disposal is booked with an approved provider; slots depend on capacity.
  4. Re-inspection confirms compliance before release.
  5. The invoice lands: inspection, treatment, transport, storage — all importer-paid.

Australia's system is procedural and appealable, but the economics are not: by the time a direction is issued, the cheap solutions are already gone.

Common Pitfalls

  • Checking BICON after buying stock. The database answers questions you needed answered before the purchase order.
  • Assuming "just a pallet" is fine. Non-stamped or bark-bearing wood is the most common single trigger.
  • Using straw, husk or plant-fibre filler. It is contamination by definition; switch fillers.
  • Copying last shipment's packing declaration. Declarations must reflect the actual container and cargo.
  • Vague parcel descriptions. "Gift" and "accessories" invite opening; accurate descriptions pass.
  • Listing seeds, plants or food items casually. These are the most-rejected B2C categories from China.
